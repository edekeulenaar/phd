# After deplatforming: the return of trace research for the study of content moderation 

When platforms were considered “intermediaries”, or conduits through which content flows unfettered [@kleisnielsenDealingDigitalIntermediaries2018], they could serve as sites for the study of user traces: online actions and behaviors interactively registered by the platforms and made available to researchers in the form of hit logs, links, likes, retweets, shares and so forth. One could observe user behavior and attitude unobtrusively, as if “in the wild” [@webbUnobtrusiveMeasuresNonreactive1966]. These measures would provide insights into collective mood and sentiments, give indicators of opinion and perhaps even capture animal spirits behind stock price or currency movements [@lazerNetworksPoliticalAttitudes2008; @lazerComputationalSocialScience2020; @wattsInfluentialsNetworksPublic2007].

But there is a certain artificiality to platform data that results from platform as well as user actions. On the platform side, there are attempts to retain users with “sticky content” to increase revenue tied to “watch time”; there are even “dark patterns” underlying user interfaces that steer users toward forms of conversion. While certain content is promoted, other kinds are demoted. Deplatforming, labeling and downranking are content moderation techniques that adversely affect the visibility of platform data. By *deplatforming*, I mean the temporary or permanent removal of content, accounts or channels from a service; by *labeling* or *flagging*, the addition of a visible warning, context or status to content that remains available; and by *demotion* or *downranking*, a reduction in visibility within search, recommendation or feed rankings without necessarily removing the content.

On the user side, there is the amplification as well as the manipulation of content, such as acquiring fake views, likes and followers as well as gaming recommendation algorithms to up-rank certain content in others’ feeds. On top of that, there is SEO (search engine optimization) and analytics-driven content production – both seed content (or its containers) to boost it. Finally, there are obfuscation techniques deployed by users that affect platform data. They may shield themselves from platform oversight by employing code words while linking to extreme material, posting mirrored images with explicit text, or setting accounts or posts to private.

Though there have been isolated studies about the function, roles and construction of each of these effects [@chandrasekharanQuarantinedExaminingEffects2021a; @rogersDeplatformingFollowingExtreme2020], there is not a clear sense of their overall significance or their effect on research that uses platform data to study all manner of societal phenomena. Social media companies provide some data. There are platform transparency reports and blog posts detailing the percentage of content taken down for hate speech and misinformation as well as by official state requests. This kind of documentation, however, tends to be considered performative “PR exercises” [@hillDefinitelyNotBusiness2024]. Especially in the case of Facebook, the transparency report (and the accompanying banner messages on CrowdTangle, Meta’s data source until August 2024) strives to steer the conversation away from the fact that low-quality (and extreme) posts receive the most engagement on the platform [@rooseFacebookWeakerWe2021].

Paradoxically, one solution to the “non-traceability” of the data behind platform transparency reports and the effects of content moderation has been to do trace research. That is, one needs trace research to answer the question of platform effects, including content moderation. As related above, this is a break from the previous use of trace research, studying user behavior as well as content engagement to gauge societal trends and sentiment.

In the field of content moderation studies, some examples of doing trace research for the study of platform content moderation are recovering traces of content removal by continually archiving posts, tweets or videos (see [Chapter 13. Modulating moderation](#/chapter-13)); following patterns of demotion by scraping the rankings of search results on YouTube or Twitter ([Chapter 12. Deplatforming, demotion and folk theories of Big Tech persecution](#/chapter-12)); or scraping labels, “context” flags and other post statuses on Twitter over time ([Chapter 8. Twitter as an accidental authority](#/chapter-8)).

The overall approach behind these studies could be described as a form of digital methods similar in style to digital forensics, in the sense that it is designed to reconstruct the scene in which platform or user data have disappeared after content moderation. We discuss six methods: (1) understanding what is likely to be moderated by charting a history of content moderation policies with the Wayback Machine; (2) reverse-engineering content moderation with the “dynamic archiving” of data susceptible to being moderated; and (3) capturing traces of moderation, either with platform metadata or by scraping flags, context labels or content rankings. We also discuss user effects, namely: (4) how users carve safe passages into alternative platforms, particularly to redirect their audiences toward content that the host platform does not allow; (5) how they create neologisms designed to confuse platform moderation; and (6) how they themselves interpret and talk about moderation.

Each of these research techniques consists in capturing whatever trace remains after content has been re-versioned, updated, over-written, suppressed or removed. As such, it puts trace research to a new use. Rather than studying user behavior by treating the platform as an intermediary, it studies platform behavior by treating users as actively moderated. It thereby constitutes a different strategy for deploying trace research. In the earlier version of trace research, one might collect platform data about users and consider the extent to which they can be cleaned to remove platform effects and other content-befouling causes (such as bots, optimized content or artificial amplification). Now, the trace research takes as its point of departure platform efforts to cleanse and police the site of rule-breaking or offending content, artfully capturing those acts. A similar strategy can be seen in censorship studies – itself a form of trace research – where for example scholars look for whatever documentation exists of text, audio or audiovisual material being edited, revised or vetted by censors [see @postCensorshipSilencingPractices1998]. In doing so, they have repurposed the medium of censorship to study various cultural processes, such as the diffusion of ideas through alternative and counterfeit publishing networks [@mellotCounterfeitPrintingAgent2007], new linguistic expressions [@burtAdministrationAestheticsCensorship1994], or even its role in the creation of historical counter-spheres, or “undergrounds”, that preceded revolutionary movements [@darntonCensorshipComparativePerspective1994].

In these examples, trace research gains a new purpose. It seeks to answer, at least in part, the question of the effects of content moderation on the content under study. In a sense, it could be seen as a necessary step prior to content analysis or trace research in its original form. Or it could be considered a research practice in itself that has as its main aim content reconstruction with a series of uses, as we discuss.

In what follows, we first acknowledge the methodological relevance of other forms of trace research, such as censorship studies, to the kind of research proposed above. We then outline methods and use cases for the study of content moderation through trace research for the study of platform effects. These include content moderation policy research; reconstructing instances of content removal, flagging and demotion; and capturing user strategies for content moderation evasion. These methods are supported by work-in-progress tools we have made available for programmatic research, which we dub “MCAT”, short for Moderation Capture and Analysis Toolkit [@dekeulenaarMCATModerationCapture2024; @MCATModerationTracking]. MCAT operationalizes the methods developed by this chapter in collaboration with Ivan Kisjes, Matus Solcany and Frank Anemaet. 

We then conclude with a discussion on the significance of (empirical) content moderation research, be it for scholars invested in digital methods or platform governance, or others tracing the modulation of speech norms in social media environments. In doing so, we also report on limitations, some of which pertain to any kind of reconstructive (web) research [@aroraUsingWaybackMachine2016], while others are due to the growing pains of “post-API” research [@perriamDigitalMethodsPostAPI2020].

# Censorship studies and other precedents of trace research

Perhaps because they observe a similar phenomenon, doing trace research on content moderation is a craft inevitably steeped in various historical equivalents, the most obvious of which is the study of censorship in literature and communication sciences. Central to these studies is an attempt at uncovering the norms of public speech management in written, spoken and visual forms [@mooreCensorship2016a], where what is left to the researcher is often only a *trace*, rather than its inner workings. 

Such studies point to at least four interventions. The first is a study of the norms and execution of censorial powers over public speech, which in historical studies have typically been identified as state and other institutional actors [see @darntonCensorshipComparativePerspective1994; @jonesCensorshipWorldEncyclopedia2001; @mooreCensorship2016a]. Others prefer to map a more ubiquitous form of “constitutive” censorship, as they depart from the assumption that the control of public speech can be found in all domains of society [@bourdieuLangagePouvoirSymbolique2001; @jansensuecurryCensorshipKnotThat1988]. In both cases, scholars have tended to rely more on direct access to the censors themselves, when available, to shed light on the norms (or ideology), the logic and organizational culture behind whichever censorial traces and effects one encounters. Readers of @darntonCensorshipComparativePerspective1994 may remember the painstaking work of interviewing German RDA censors about their motives (and their humorous objections to the term “censorship”), how they captured, processed and edited documents prior to publication, and which censorial techniques were used for each media. 

When access to censors is impossible (or complementary), scholars rely on *what is left* to see after censorship. These are *traces* in the proper sense of the word. They appear as marks on the object itself, as revisions prior to publication, obfuscation techniques in post-published objects (for example, black boxes and bleeps), or in the very absence of something known to have existed prior to interception [@mullerCensorshipCulturalRegulation2004, p. 4]. There are also visible *effects*, particularly the carving of alternative pathways to dissemination. Burt [-@burtAdministrationAestheticsCensorship1994], for example, identified instances in which eighteenth-century British playwrights would effectively transpose the meanings, innuendos, or effects of censored terms into less suspicious expressions. Of course, one does not need to look so far back in history to find evidence of such displacements; in these and many other historical contexts, others have mapped the wide constellations of alternative publication markets “whether abroad or in the underground” [@mullerCensorshipCulturalRegulation2004, p. 5].

Whatever the method used in censorial studies, it is hampered by the same difficulty long identified in content moderation studies: that traces of moderation are partial, ephemeral and often kept inaccessible for systematic data collection. As such, these methods need not change a great deal when applied to online data. Let us recapitulate the above-mentioned methods as an order, or “stack”, that begins with looking at top-down regulation, trickles down into constitutive censorial practices (destroying, obfuscating or otherwise editing information), then their effects (transmutation of censored language, etc.) and finally the formation of alternative publishing methods. One could identify a very similar order in social media platform ecologies, as displayed in [the moderation stack below](#^figure-1). From a top-down level, content moderation policies are studied as “official records” for what social media platforms – institutional forces in their own right – consider as problematic speech and why, as well as how they manage that speech with a variety of reinforcement mechanisms [see @gorwaAlgorithmicContentModeration2020; @katzenbachPlatformGovernanceArchives2023]. Policies can serve as guides that indicate what is problematic and how that is moderated; with them, one can design the right queries and collect the right data and metadata to monitor content moderation practices, such as deplatforming, demotion, flagging and others. At the bottom, one looks at user strategies for circumventing moderation, including migrating their content to alternative platforms [@rogersDeplatformingFollowingExtreme2020], or creating an interdependent fringe-to-mainstream network of more-or-less censored content.

Each component of this “moderation stack” will be detailed in what follows (**[Figure 1](#^figure-1)**).

# Content moderation policy analysis

Until now, the most popular method for the study of content moderation relies on what is chiefly available for empirical inquiry: content moderation policies and related documentation. In this context, content moderation policies have been examined as “performative” attempts by platforms to signal to legislators and advertisers their grip on an emerging public scandal [@hillDefinitelyNotBusiness2024], particularly after the so-called “techlash” of 2018 [@theeconomistTechlashAmazonFacebook2018]. Others may study policies as primary materials for histories of concepts, techniques or “platform mechanisms” related to content moderation [@vandijckPlatformMechanisms2018]. And others may systematically archive platform policies as primary sources for a variety of platform histories, particularly platform governance [@katzenbachPlatformGovernanceArchives2023].

Following in the footsteps of Gillespie [-@gillespietarletonCustodiansInternet2018], such studies tend to rely on critical platform discourse analysis. DeCook et al. [-@decookSafeHarmGovernance2022b] use the Wayback Machine to look at how Facebook, Twitter and YouTube define “harm” over time in relevant policies published throughout 2021. Jiang et al. [-@jiangCharacterizingCommunityGuidelines2020] compare policy documents in search of commonalities in Facebook, Twitter and YouTube definitions of objectionable behavior. And Paßmann et al. [-@passmannHealthyCommunitiesToxic2023b] use platform policy pages, blog posts and other corporate updates to find how Disqus, one of the pioneers in blog comment software, came to be increasingly preoccupied with “toxic communities”.

Taking a step back, policies can be used as maps from which to retrace a variety of other content moderation interventions. Such maps would indicate: (1) the kinds of content deemed problematic and why; (2) how that content (or its users) is moderated (removed, suspended, flagged or other); and, above all, (3) the context in which a kind of content was problematized and a platform took action against it (or absolved it from moderation, as happened when Musk took over Twitter in 2023). In turn, each of these components will indicate new paths forward, namely: what kind of content to collect from each platform, and with what queries; what “traces” of moderation to collect alongside this content; and whatever other relevant data one finds with such queries, such as evidence of user moderation through keyword obfuscation or out-links to alternative platforms. Each of these steps is described below.

First, what a platform considers to be problematic, be it “abusive behavior” or “borderline content”, becomes the source of a set of queries to study removal. Policies may provide more or less specific definitions and examples, or even paraphrase problematic verbiage used by sanctioned users. Whatever their level of precision, one can research the legal, political and online context behind each policy, inferring from circumstantial information or online controversies what the platform could mean. Examples include public harassment scandals informing “abusive behavior” policies such as Gamergate [@jeongHistoryTwittersRules2016], journalistic reporting of YouTube’s conspiracy and “alternative influence” networks [@lewisAlternativeInfluenceBroadcasting2018] as a background to YouTube’s targeting of “borderline content” [@youtubeYouTubeMisinformationHow2021] and ever-granular forms of hate speech [@youtubeOurOngoingWork2019], and, of course, public health crises leading to COVID-19 misinformation policy updates [@aljazeeraTwitterPermanentlyBan2021].

Second, policies often describe how problematic content is moderated, again with greater or lesser precision. A specific claim relating to COVID-19 misinformation may be labeled, while more conspiratorial variants may be demoted down search or recommendation results. Whatever the reinforcement technique, its policy description can inform researchers what metadata to scrape or collect through an API. The idea behind this method is to eventually “reverse-engineer” each moderation technique, as their effect can be retraced in various data and metadata points in a given dataset. For example, one could reverse-engineer some parts of YouTube’s demotion of borderline content by collecting video search rankings over time. Or, one could compare the availability of a given post or video at different moments to find evidence of suspension.

In combining all of the information inferred from a policy document – queries for data collection, collected data and moderation traces – we go on to study the general process of content moderation within broader platform events. Each of the above-mentioned steps is detailed below.

## Collecting policy data

One of the first questions to pose when analyzing moderation policies concerns the scale of the issue and the documents to select. One may wish to retrace the moderation of specific content (electoral misinformation) or a larger issue (harassment or harm). One could select corresponding policy documents (e.g., Twitter’s Electoral Integrity or Abusive behavior policies), but quickly notice that policies will have merged or changed titles and content, making it impossible to construct a linear genealogy. Twitter’s “Hateful conduct” and “Abusive behavior”, for example, have morphed from relatively neutral ideas of “technical harm” (such as spam or “technical abuse”) into various types of social abuse. Some content has become less and less tolerated over the years, while other kinds simply had their reach “limited” (see [Chapter 8. Twitter as an accidental authority](#/chapter-8)).

One direct way of selecting and collecting policy documents is to use the Platform Governance Archives (v. 1 and 2) of Katzenbach et al. [-@katzenbachPlatformGovernanceArchives2023]. Such sources are particularly reliable because they are systematically collected, cleaned (i.e., non-textual data is removed) and stored in PDF, HTML and markdown formats. Both archives classify policies per “types”, which are privacy, terms of service and community guidelines. As of mid-2024, however, they do not discriminate policies within these policy types. This means that one cannot chart a history of a single policy (or collection of relevant policies) without manually separating policy type documents or using a trained classifier. To facilitate this task, the Platform Governance Archives and MCAT offer a list of all current Terms of Service, Privacy and Community Guidelines policy links from all platforms. One can then place these in the Wayback Machine Link Ripper [@digitalmethodsinitiativeInternetArchiveWayback2022], a browser-based research utility that retrieves archived links and available snapshots for a supplied URL or domain, and download each version using a command line. The utility accelerates discovery rather than guaranteeing completeness: its output remains bounded by what the Internet Archive captured, by exclusions from the archive and by changes in URL structure, so dates, query parameters and missing intervals should be retained with the resulting dataset.

There are ways to collect policies manually, though they are not always straightforward. One solution is to get out- and in-links from a platform homepage, filter results to only general policy URLs, and then crawl in- and out-links from those. The reason why casting a wide net is advisable is because policy links will often change structures over time (for example, https://www.twitter.com/help will become https://help.twitter.com at an arbitrary point in time), and so cannot be used to trace a complete history. Instead, one can paste domain URLs in the Wayback Network Per Year, which is a simple tool that scrapes yearly in- and out-links from the Wayback Machine archive of a set of URLs. From its results, we can then pick policy URLs. These will typically contain mentions of “help”, “support”, “rules”, “safety”, “terms”, “policies”, “community guidelines” or “blog” for direct content moderation updates. By crawling at least two link iterations — in- and out-links from homepages, in- and out-links of those links, and perhaps their own in- and out-links — we eventually find the URLs of specific policies. The end result is a long list of Wayback Machine URLs that can be filtered to only include relevant policy pages.

We can also use the Link Ripper to crawl the internal links of policy-related pages found in Wayback links of a platform domain, though the large quantity of results may make this task difficult. In [Chapter 13. Modulating moderation](#/chapter-13), scraping in- and out-links from all archived twitter.com links resulted in 20,731 archived links for 31 unique policy pages. Besides specific policies like Hateful Conduct and Abusive Behavior, results included blog posts announcing policy updates that may not always be written into formal policy documents. In practice, this meant using the Wayback Machine Link Ripper to collect the archived URLs of a platform homepage (e.g., twitter.com), and then crawling those URLs to find internal policy links. In doing so, one can also record the number of changes made to each policy version, including by using textdiff functions available on MCAT [@MCATModerationTracking]. Before diving into the content of each policy, these results already display the changing structure of a platform’s policy organization, and therefore some of the company’s internal organizational changes.

## Understanding what a platform finds problematic

In what follows, we can examine platform policies with either a distant or close reading approach. A distant reading is quantitative, consisting in analyzing the number of changes one or several policy documents have undergone by number of edits (per day, month, year) or the length of those edits (in the form of a word, a sentence, parts of the graphical UI, such as a chunk of HTML code, and so on). The number of edits can be computed with a textdiff routine or with `difflib`, a built-in Python module that allows users to compare data sets, such as daily instances of archived policies. The results, visualized in the form of a line, Gantt or any other chart, can indicate resource investment per policy or the larger issues a platform has sought to address. One example for YouTube is **[Figure 2](#^figure-2)**. In it, we see significant changes in 2018 (following the introduction of the Santa Clara Principles) as well as a “complexification” [@passmannHealthyCommunitiesToxic2023b] of content moderation into a myriad of case-specific policies.

A manual and more meticulous way of collecting such information consists in reading every policy, extracting and placing specific components from the text into a repository, such as a spreadsheet. How to go about this procedure depends on the structural consistency of the policy documentation. Given their quasi-legal nature, policy documents are written in somewhat predictable ways; they open with a rationale for why they are enforced and continue with what is not tolerated and the corresponding sanctions, i.e., enforcement techniques. If there has been little change over time, one may consider using an automated technique to highlight the changes, such as counting the number of times a word is mentioned in policies over time (e.g., “suspend”). However, because policies may be written in a variety of ways, one would in most cases undertake a manual annotation of key sections of a policy.

For each policy version – per day, month, year or other timeframe – one retains a policy category (“Twitter Rules & Policies”); its name (“Abusive behavior”), prohibited content(s) (“Unwanted sexual advances”, etc.); examples, if applicable (“Solicitation of sexual acts”); and the enforcement techniques assigned to each content. ## Query design and dynamic archiving

Having studied the contents of policy documents, we have been able to determine what the platform considers to be problematic, how it defines problematic content over time (and in the context of socio-political events) and what enforcement techniques (and thereby degrees of sanctioning) were applied to each. It gives researchers a sense of each platform’s “history of discipline”. It informs us about what has come to be intolerable and perhaps why, and the kind of language that begins to disappear (or be “shadowed”) from the platform under stricter enforcement conditions.

From there, we can begin reconstructing the contextual and material circumstances in which content has been moderated. We have mentioned how the first step in a reconstruction is to capture what the platform finds problematic. This consists of effectively translating a platform’s definition of problematic content into a query, which can be a keyword that likely leads to finding that content, or a set of users one knows to be targeted, such as “extreme personalities” [@rogersDeplatformingFollowingExtreme2020]. Should the policy be against “misleading COVID information” – defined by YouTube as statements that contradict “authoritative sources” – the query becomes a variety of terms that can likely retrieve claims contrary to statements by the World Health Organization or local equivalents such as “#coronahoax”. Occasionally, policy documents provide specific examples that can be taken directly as queries. Both Twitter and YouTube laid out increasingly unambiguous examples of what amounted to “misleading COVID information”, going so far as to specify the tonality of a misleading sentence (see [Chapter 13. Modulating moderation](#/chapter-13)). Should the policy be against “hate speech” or “hateful conduct”, one can rely on an expert list of hateful terms (for example, non-ambiguous terms from [@hatebaseHatebase2022] or [@peetersSalvagingInternetHate2020]). The key to designing queries lies in replicating as closely as possible the terminology used by the platform to describe problematic content. To do so, one considers the context surrounding a given policy, be it whatever is occurring within a platform at a given moment in time or public controversies targeting platform content moderation.

Once queries are designed, data collection may begin. We call this process “dynamic archiving”, because it consists in continuously archiving content that is likely to be moderated before and/or during data collection. Typically, digital methods and computational social science research projects perform one or several iterations of data collection, and the analysis is based on the data and metadata from those time frames. This procedure can be limiting for the study of content moderation because it does not capture possible alterations of content – be it of a post, its user’s status, its ranking in search or recommendation results, and other by-products of moderation – unless one collects the data anew and compares results. Some alterations of note are suspensions, which may be permanent or temporary (meaning a user may disappear and reappear later); demotion, which may affect both search and recommendation results; and labels, which may be removed once policies end. For example, Twitter’s old COVID-19 misleading information policy is no longer in effect as of November 2023.

Dynamic archiving must adapt to different platform access regimes. It could once be done on X with the Twitter Capture and Analysis Toolkit (TCAT) [@borraProgrammedMethodDeveloping2014] if a user retained API access, or with a scraper if not. The fact that TCAT collected Tweets daily allowed one to create contemporaneous data archives of “running” Tweet moderation. With this archive, one could later find which Tweets had been removed by comparing the statuses of present-day and archived Tweets, together with their engagement, ranking and other relevant metadata. The equivalent process can be done on YouTube with youtube-dl [@garciagonzalezYoutubedl2022], an open-source command-line program used for scraping content and metadata, including channel names, channel IDs, video IDs, video comments, video transcripts, engagement (views, likes and dislikes), search result rankings and video status (e.g., “This video has been removed due to copyright.”). One can set it up to collect this information by numbers of hours, days or months. API-based alternatives are the YouTube Data Tools [@riederYouTubeDataTools2019]; for Facebook and Instagram, CrowdTangle; and, for smaller platforms, multi-platform collection tools such as 4CAT [@peeters4CATCaptureAnalysis2018].

# Reconstructing the scene of content moderation 

What one chooses to capture with any one of these tools depends on the moderation technique under analysis. There are at least three types of techniques from which to derive metadata: labeling, suspension or removal (“deplatforming”), and demotion. We will outline each of these below.

## Removal and suspension

By “deplatforming”, one refers to the temporary or permanent expulsion of content or users from a given platform. On X, deplatforming may refer to the temporary or permanent suspension of users, or the removal of posts. On YouTube, channels and videos may be suspended under a conditional “strike system”, which imposes suspensions for one or more weeks depending on the infringement. We also find that channels are demonetized prior to removal (which may arguably count as a type of deplatforming) or are removed altogether. Specific videos may no longer be available for a variety of policy infringements.

Traces of deplatforming may be found via platform APIs or user interfaces. As of June 2023, there are GitHub repositories that look for user statuses with user_lookup and other functions [@bruno78BasicTwitterApi2016]. But these functions usually do not specify the reasons why content has been removed, returning instead information about a post or user being online or not. More specific statuses include prompts in YouTube video thumbnails, such as “This video has been removed for infringing the hate speech policy”, or disclaimers in Tweets and/or users one tries but fails to access: “Sorry, this post is no longer available!”. To collect these, one can set up a web scraper, such as Selenium, to extract the specific sections containing the page status of a post, user, channel or video (see, for example, [MCAT scrapers](https://amsterdam-humanities-labs.github.io/MCAT/)). The users or posts no longer available can be captured through dynamic archiving and comparing older and newer versions of a dataset, whereupon one will note that some content is no longer available. Once that content is identified, its status can be scraped from its page.

In some cases – particularly on X – content and user removals may not be definite. As noted above, several policies stipulate that suspensions can be temporary or permanent, depending on the severity of a violation. These more intermittent forms of deplatforming invite a continuous tracing of post and user removals. One can keep scraping the status of a selection of posts, at chosen intervals, and find that some posts that were initially online were later suspended and then back online again, or vice versa.

## Labels

The strategy of scraping content or user statuses can also be used to collect content labels. Labels were used especially during the times of the COVID-19 pandemic and Twitter’s World Leaders policies (**[[#^figure-5|Figure 5]]**) to add nuance or context to “misleading” information, particularly by users with political mandates (such as ex-US President Donald Trump), or Tweets whose “misleading” statements may become more or less acceptable depending on external circumstances, such as the development of “authoritative” knowledge on the COVID-19 virus. Above, we mentioned that labels may change over time, particularly when the policy instating them is suspended. In this case, one can use an automated scraper to collect post, video or channel labels every so often, and then compare them over time. An interesting metric for comparing labels is engagement and rankings, as the late Twitter tended to deactivate sharing and up-ranking for some types of labeled content.

## Demotion

Demotion may refer to reducing the visibility of problematic content in user interfaces in a variety of ways, depending on the platform. YouTube describes demotion as up-ranking or “raising” authoritative content in search results, including news media and other trusted institutional sources [@youtubeFourRsResponsibility2021]. It also claims that “borderline contents” are buried in search and recommendation results intentionally [@youtubeContinuingOurWork2019]. Twitter claimed that problematic posts may be ranked downward in search results and replies, and may no longer appear in promotional emails or feeds, or be shared among users [@twitterSpecificInstancesWhen2017]. Regardless of their specific methods, both platforms aim to downrank problematic content using two principal metrics: visibility and engagement.

These two metrics are found in API documentation or user interfaces in slightly different ways. Rankings may be found in YouTube search results or related videos. Post search results and reply rankings may be obtained through scraping, using for example the Web Data Research Assistant browser plug-in [@WebDataResearch]. Engagement, on the other hand, can be found in the form of video likes, comments and views, or post replies, quotes and impressions – a reach metric recently introduced by X.

To examine X demotion (or a consequence of such), one can compare the rankings of posts in search and reply rankings over time, since demotion consists in hiding or down-ranking posts in order to reduce engagement with problematic content. To do so, rankings, engagement and post content can be collected with a scraper every so often, as done in [MCAT](https://amsterdam-humanities-labs.github.io/MCAT/).

## Contextualization

The final step in this procedure is to contextualize instances of labeling, deplatforming or demotion. The idea is to understand how policy implementations were made in the context of external or online events. The researcher can refer to the information extracted from relevant platform policies and date the implementations of enforcement measures in their case studies. Here, for example, we can keep track of all the updates of YouTube’s hate speech (and related) policies to make sense of which content from a dataset of political videos was targeted, and when (**[Figure 6](#^figure-6)**).

# Capturing user effects

What can one look for when examining moderation-related “user effects”? To date, some have documented a few ways in which users seek to obfuscate keywords that may have them banned, such as out-linking their accounts or controversial content to alternative platforms [@rogersDeplatformingFollowingExtreme2020], or countering suspicions of “shadowbanning” with content optimization techniques (see [Chapter 12. Deplatforming, demotion and folk theories of Big Tech persecution](#/chapter-12)). First, we will focus on methods to trace how users “replatform” banned content to alternative platforms, and link them back to their original accounts. Then we will discuss a method to detect ways in which users obfuscate compromising keywords, and, finally, examine how users interpret content moderation.

## Links to alternative platforms

In datasets containing data susceptible to moderation, one will often find traces of users seeking to guide their audiences toward less moderated alternatives like BitChute, Rumble or Telegram. One of the ways to find such traces is to filter URLs from one’s dataset, particularly in locations where they are used as promotional content. On YouTube, these may be a channel’s profile or video descriptions, while on X – until December 2022 [@mehtaTwitterBansPosting2022] – they had been a user’s profile description. One can visualize results in the form of an alluvial diagram, showing the “source” platform – where the user is posting from – to a “target” platform – where the user is redirecting their audiences. For example, YouTube channels involved in COVID-19 conspiracies between April and October 2020 linked to a variety of alternative platforms [@scottBigTechsTrying2021]. In other cases, users link to alternative platforms to point to content that cannot be hosted in more moderated ones. In **[Figure 7](#^figure-7)**, for example, we see how users involved in the Brazilian Congress riots of 8 January 2023 frequently linked evidence of electoral fraud and strategies to invade Brasilia’s congressional square to Telegram channels and “backup” information hosting sources, including Google Docs.

## Keyword obfuscation

Keyword obfuscation consists in altering a compromising term to prevent platforms from automatically detecting and sanctioning one’s content. Examples have been found throughout the COVID-19 pandemic, when users were compelled to use more discreet expressions of distrust concerning the nature of the virus and official guidelines for protection [@tutersDeepStatePhobia2022a]. One way to find such expressions is to use natural language processing, particularly word2vec [@goldbergWord2vecExplainedDeriving2014]. Word2vec is a machine learning technique that, provided with a large enough dataset (or at least one million rows), can find terms that are used in the same or similar context as a given query.

## Mining for user comments on moderation

To examine how users interpret content moderation, one can filter a dataset with mentions of platforms or synonyms and execute a series of analyses that provide a “distant” or “close” reading of results. Some of these “distant reading” devices are word trees – a graphical version of keyword-in-context [@wattenbergWordTreeInteractive2008] — and subject-verb-object sentence graphs [@milajevsIRMeetsNLP2015]. In an example outlined below, user comments were extracted using the following keywords: “deplatform*”, “shadowbann*”, “demot*”, “demonetis*” or “demonetiz*”, “suspend*”, “remov*”, “cancel*”, “modera*”, and “banned”. One way to visualize results is to paste all extracted sentences into a word tree and select a platform name as a root sentence.

A more complex method, informed by [@tangherliniAutomatedPipelineDiscovery2020], is to use an ensemble of combined natural language processing techniques that consist in extracting syntactic and semantic elements of conspiracy theories from “noisy” social media posts and visualize them as storytelling networks. In one case, we reconstruct subject-verb-object relations by extracting the relations between named entities, filtering relations based on their reference to conspiratorial narratives and then filtering resulting networks based on their relationship to COVID-19 conspiracy themes. We can do this by implementing a Python word2vec library [@Word2vecWrapperGoogle], which maps the n-grammatical relations between words through neural networking algorithms. As above, basic subjects include “youtube”, “google”, “big tech”, “facebook” and “twitter”, while verbs are associated with moderation.

# MCAT

MCAT, the Moderation Capture and Analysis Toolkit, consolidates these procedures into an instrument [@dekeulenaarMCATModerationCapture2024; @MCATModerationTracking]. It is research software in the sense given to the term by the Twitter Capture and Analysis Toolkit [@borraProgrammedMethodDeveloping2014] and its successor 4CAT [@peeters4CATCaptureAnalysis2022]: a “programmed method”, where the methodological commitments of a research program are written into the tool so that the choices made in collecting and analyzing data are explicit, repeatable and open to inspection. The difference is that while data collection tools like 4CAT capture what platforms make available, MCAT seeks to capture what they make unavailable — namely, moderation traces that can be collected, dated and compared.

To do so, MCAT relies on — for lack of a better word — “retrospective data”. It must be fed by data collected from other platforms, via other access points (be those APIs, agents or tools like 4CAT), to then detect modifications imposed on that data. Concretely, one uploads a spreadsheet of URLs from any prior collection, from which MCAT loads the page in a browser for scraping post and user statuses. The reason, as above, is that APIs tend to report whether a post or user exists but not why it has *ceased* to. Such reasons are given in the interface, and must thus always adapt to its language and structure. As methods in censorship and moderation studies suggest, a moderation analysis tool must thus constantly adapt to the conditions in which traces appear.

Besides finding such traces, it must also historicize their appearance — what we once called “dynamic archiving”. On MCAT, a moderation capture project may be set to repeat every so many minutes, hours or days, and each run is compared with the one immediately before it. This turns a snapshot into a history: content that is suspended and then restored, restricted and then removed, or live throughout while its neighbors disappear, becomes visible as a sequence rather than as a single observation. **[Figure 8](#^figure-8)** shows a project of this kind: fifty YouTube videos on alleged fraud in the 2020 United States election, checked at four-minute intervals, of which some remain live, one has been set to private and several are “unavailable” with no reason given. 

![Figure 8](images/Pasted%20image%2020260909183902.png)

**Figure 8.** *MCAT tracking the availability of fifty YouTube videos on alleged electoral fraud in the 2020 United States election. Each run records a state, the platform’s own notice, a timestamp and a screenshot for every URL, and reports what changed since the previous run.* Full image [here](https://edekeulenaar.github.io/phd/images/Pasted%20image%2020260909183902.png). ^figure-8

A third component concerns policies, or the top of the moderation stack. Where the procedure described earlier in this chapter reconstructs a policy’s history from Wayback Machine snapshots, MCAT draws on the Open Terms Archive [@OpenTermsArchive], a public initiative that continuously captures the terms, guidelines and policies of online services and records each version as it changes (and underlies the Platform Governance Archive). This plugs MCAT into a running source of policy captures. MCAT, in turn, offers an analysis interface that allows one to select a platform and a policy and compare how it changed between two dates, using textdiff. The point of holding policies and statuses in one instrument is the step we have called “contextualization”, where one asks what a platform considers problematic and how it moderates that content in practice. This then informs data collection and the capture of the moderation practices applied to it in a given time. 

MCAT has limitations of a particular kind. One is that each platform imposes scraping detection logics from which a user must be protected. This requires the tool to be downloaded into one’s local machine, where the user may also interact with it via an agent’s codex system. Another, as above, is that moderation traces change over time, requiring constant updates to capture methods. But with local tailoring available, one could consider the need to adapt as constitutive of the method. An instrument for studying moderation has to adapt to however it is manifested, and often against the terms of moderation itself. What MCAT proposes, then, is a *documented* capture, where the state of a post, the notice that justified it, and the moment it was observed are retained as the conditions in which moderated data is visible. 

# Addendum: AI alignment

The methods above assume a platform that moderates content after it has been posted and, in doing so, leaves marks on the interface. What becomes of them when the moderating agent is a language model? The question is twofold because AI systems are increasingly moderation mechanisms in their own right, in the form of the classifiers and moderation APIs that score text for “harm” before a human or a platform acts on it [@riederFabricsMachineModeration2021; @openaiModeration2024; @inanLlamaGuardLLMbaseda]. They are also sites where moderation is *applied*, in the sense that a model’s own outputs are governed by norms trained into it. What equivalence can one find with moderation as known in social media studies? 

Should one take a step back into the theoretical framework proposed in [Chapter 1. Censorship and moderation](#/chapter-1), moderation can be considered a speech management system used to govern plurivocal information environments. It must facilitate the circulation of information within normative boundaries that aim to guarantee social and epistemic cohesion. In this role, its use of enforcement techniques acts as levers that condition the circulation (and thus reproduction) of information based on normative criteria. This may be a common denominator with AI systems — specifically LLMs — where *alignment* consists in *steering* the (re)generation of information based on set normative criteria. 

Alignment refers to the set of techniques by which a model is made to behave according to the values, norms or preferences its developer specifies [@gabrielArtificialIntelligenceValues2020; @jiAIAlignmentComprehensive2024; @christianAlignmentProblemMachine2021c]. Though its use may not always enjoy universal consensus [@kasirzadehPluralityValuePluralism; @schusterMoralDisagreementLimits2025], alignment describes the effort by which AI models are steered into generating information in one or another way based on a number of trained norms. There are prompts that cause models not to generate any information at all and others that lead to them using more or less predictable discursive techniques in support or opposition to one or another norm. This applies not just in chatbots, but in the integration of models in a variety of information-sorting techniques across the Web, such as search and recommendation. 

With alignment comes the art of translating “intentions and values” into machine behavior [@jiAIAlignmentContemporary2025], relative to how different actors specify those items in time, space and dialogue [@cannonEnactiveApproachValue2022; @pappasHumanValueAlignment2025, pp. 24–30]. The practical difference with content moderation may lie in the specific affordances left for external actors to deliberate and input their own values into AI models — and not just at the stage of policy design [@peterDecentralisingLLMAlignment2025, p. 1989]. We speak indeed of interventions across the AI “lifecycle” [@kuehnertWhoWhatHow2025], from the specification of risks and values (i.e., a form of policy design); data brokerage and curation; training methods (reinforcement learning and constitutional AI among many others); and benchmarking and other forms of more or less external criteria for training and evaluation (see **[Figure 9](#^figure-9)**). 

**Figure 9.** *The AI alignment stack: the definition of risks and virtues, their training and evaluation.* ^figure-9

Much literature on value alignment has tended to focus on areas long visited by content moderation studies, particularly which alignment values each company picks and defines in policies and model cards. But while the object of analysis may be similar, moderation and alignment policies differ at least *discursively*. Terms of Service and content moderation policy documents tend to declare the hard limitations of what platforms can host, thus taking aim at specific kinds of content or content-based behavior [@gorwaAlgorithmicContentModeration2020]. In alignment, one departs from the observation that AI is a technology with exponentially unknowable consequences of high impact in nearly all sectors of society. This means that alignment is written *prospectively*, in a deontological and aspirational sense. AI charters, constitutions, model cards and policy documents ideate what AIs ought to be as ideal technologies for users and societies. In conjunction with benchmarks, they also prescribe specific scenarios for how they ought to behave against existing and potential risks. Unlike moderation documents, then, alignment is best seen as “process” [@gabrielArtificialIntelligenceValues2020] rather than a finite classification of problematic objects.  

Empirically, this means casting a wider net onto company policies. We no longer speak of moderation documents stipulating what users may and may not do with an AI service, but model cards, canonical principles, charters or “constitutions”, announcements, repositories, and any other trace that explains — like a moderation document — *what is defined* as problematic information (a risk) or an ideal conduct (or virtue) by which to train an AI. Discursively, principles may possess some of the same PR functions as policy documents, but with the added necessity to prove an alignment project or philosophy. They stipulate the finite values by which a model is trained, its red lines and its normative horizon, given its changing capacities. In practical terms, one may see it as the root repository of norms, definitions, scenarios and metrics by which specific virtues and risks are trained.

In model cards, one can see relatively standardized documents that report how a model was trained [@mitchellModelCardsModel2019b]. The components are usually a stated training objective, including the risk-mitigation strategies and virtues companies want to embed in their models; the training process, meaning what training was chosen, with whom and how; and the benchmarks used to verify the effectiveness of the training. From this document, one can see which risks a company focuses on mitigating, as well as which virtues to counter-balance them. One can also find the training processes used to ingrain a desired behavior, as well as the company’s choice of external benchmark criteria to legitimize its training process. 

All of these documents can be used to illustrate the normative or sentimental education of a model. As above, one can extrapolate such findings into other alignment-related processes. Indeed, we may map a company-specific alignment process within a broader constellation of alignment processes (**[Figure 10](#^figure-10)**), or “stack”. At its top sit legislation and the broader conventions to which AI is increasingly made answerable. There are also model distributors, like Hugging Face, that render some models more or less accessible based on their own normative criteria [@gorwaModeratingModelMarketplaces2024b]. There are then company-specific policies, whose model cards, reports, usage policies and “constitutions” define what a company designates as risks and virtues. These, in turn, enframe an alignment process: they define expert consultation and red teaming partners; dataset cleaning; and the learning procedures by which desirable conduct is taught. Below that is enforcement as it appears to the user: the generation of “aligned” speech, the chain of thought that illustrates and justifies it, and the content warnings that may block one or another use. Then come the user circumventions, be these jailbreaking prompts or the alternative models and platforms to which users migrate. At the bottom lie the AI infrastructures – cloud services, content delivery networks, domain registrars, Internet service providers – whose own policies determine where a model may be hosted at all.

![Figure 10](images/AI%20moderation%20stack.png)

**Figure 10.** *The AI moderation stack: where and how to look at value alignment.* Full image [here](https://edekeulenaar.github.io/phd/images/AI%20moderation%20stack.png). ^figure-10

[Chapter 5. LLMs and the generation of moderate speech](#/chapter-5) focuses on the enforcement layer of this stack. It looks for the *enforcement techniques* used by models when prompted by questions that trigger their moderation sub-routines. A model tends to output different kinds of discourses based on what the prompt does to it. To trigger a moderation subroutine, one can derive prompts from a space with a wider spectrum of permissibility than a model permits. Reddit or 4chan may serve this purpose well, particularly Q&A subreddits interested in actively controversial topics. 

As such, collecting prompts from different platforms has a second use. Reddit estimates *controversy* as the balance between the upvotes and downvotes a question receives. LLM models tend to estimate *risk* through internal moderation APIs that score a prompt from 0 to 1 for the likelihood that it causes a variety of “harms”. The two measures capture different normative logics, such that prompting models with top controversial prompts helps identify what a model may and may not talk about — i.e., its relative normative boundaries. 

This method also shows how discursive techniques are distributed. Where a question carries a high risk score, one sees which technique the model reaches for — a refusal, or a refusal with a normative justification — and thus how closely a technique is tied to a risk estimation. Where a question is controversial but not risky, one sees what a model falls back on when its moderation training gives it no clear instruction: agnostic, diplomatic or academic speech. This exercise can be repeated for historical analysis. As models are updated, deprecated or replaced, running the same prompts through their successors gives a record of how these techniques evolve.

# Conclusions: the return of trace research

Trace research, or the deployment of data from user activity on platforms as indicators of societal trends and sentiment, has been critiqued because of the manner in which platforms boost content and thereby “manage user behavior” [@qinUserBehaviorRetrieval2020]. Platforms have a vested interest in shaping the way their users interact with content, for they benefit from greater engagement and longer watch times. Traces also suffer from other platform effects such as content demotion and user suspension or removal. On top of that, users, for their part, may engage in reputation engineering practices such as the acquisition of fake followers or content placement optimization driven by analytics as well as SEO (search engine optimization) practices. They may obfuscate their interests through the use of code words and manipulated images as well as privacy controls that set posts and accounts to private. All of these measures deplete the value of the traces left behind by users and taken up by researchers.

As we have argued, trace research, however, could be put to use to study the very problems outlined above. We propose a return to the analytical practice of studying traces by using it to detail the platform effects on online data in the first instance. That is, we deploy traces in order to study how the platform and its users are affecting the data available on the platform.

We discuss six methods for the study of content moderation through trace research. The first is a method to chart the evolution of content moderation policy using the Wayback Machine of the Internet Archive. The second concerns content removal. Through what we term “dynamic archiving” we retain data susceptible to removal and demotion, in a sense “reverse-engineering” content moderation. The third demonstrates how to capture overt moderation traces, including flags and labels. These three analytical procedures are aimed at the platform side, while three others focus on user practices that affect data. The fourth traces the movement of content and users to alternative platforms where moderation is less stringent. The fifth captures the wording users employ to skirt platform moderation. The sixth mines user commentary to establish how moderation is itself interpreted by those subject to it.

In all, these approaches make use of traces of the effects of content moderation on platform data. They seek to make visible practices of content demotion and removal as well as user suspension and deplatforming. They capture outward markers, such as flags. They show how users affect content availability through techniques that obfuscate or redirect audiences to alternative spaces. Each of these approaches may be deployed to study content moderation in its own right, or, as said, to gauge its impact on the trace data that is left for researchers to study societal trends and sentiment. MCAT consolidates them into a single instrument, so that the availability of content, the notices that account for it and the policies under which it was moderated can be captured and dated together; and, as the addendum suggests, the same disposition extends to AI alignment, where moderation leaves its traces not on the interface but in the documents that define it, the answers that enact it and the workarounds by which users evade it.
