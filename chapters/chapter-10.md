# **Chapter 10**
# Platform ruptures and the formation of online counter spheres

From 4chan to Truth Social, “alt-tech” spheres are not new spaces, but they have nevertheless challenged the view that the Web has consolidated around only a handful of increasingly hegemonic social media platforms. Since 2018, different approaches to content moderation — together with profoundly contentious debates about the nature of political speech norms — have produced a more refracted ecosystem, where “alt-tech” platforms have emerged as both alternatives and competitors to dominant spaces. This constitutes a paradoxical situation because, until very recently, users inhabited a thoroughly “platformized” environment [@helmondPlatformizationWebMaking2015]: a landscape marked by the hegemony of a small number of platforms that operated with broadly similar moderation norms. They are today confronted with the fragmentation of these same places into a multiplicity of competing and ideologically differentiated spaces.

Looking back, there have already been other tectonic shifts in the organization of the Web. One of the most discussed was the “fragmentation” of broadcasters as points of “common information sources” into personalized and sometimes stochastic clusters of hyperlinks [@sunsteinRepubliccom2002]. The later era of “platformization” [@helmondPlatformizationWebMaking2015] was marked by the consolidation of these fragmented sources into a few dominant intermediaries. At that point, the problem appeared reversed: users no longer consumed information that was overly dispersed, but consumed instead _too much of the same content_, immersed in echo chambers, filter bubbles and other metaphors of informational homogeneity. Platformization was also infrastructural, as it subjected an expanding range of societal sectors to the logic of “platform mechanisms” [@vandijckPlatformMechanisms2018]. 

This new fragmentation has its own particularities. Fragmented platforms are frequently the result of fractures caused by content moderation decisions, particularly the “deplatforming” of thousands of users or pieces of content [@rogersDeplatformingFollowingExtreme2020]. In response, these platforms remodel themselves in the image of alternative speech norms and ideals, often articulated around notions of freedom of expression and user autonomy [@oteroWeNotMarshal2025]. The phenomenon is visible across multiple areas of the Web, including wikis, social media, and video hosting. Other times, fragmentation occurs *within* existing platforms. The transformation of Twitter into X exemplifies this, and it has been accompanied by the return of formerly deplatformed users and the exile of others toward Bluesky or Mastodon (see [Chapter 14. From Twitter to X](#/chapter-14)).

Studies on alt-tech ecosystems have concentrated on describing what makes these spaces different. Alt-tech spheres have been described as sites for alternative subcultures [@zuckermanDeplatformingOurWay2021]; as venues for alternative moderation conventions and communication affordances [@rogersDeplatformingFollowingExtreme2020]; or as infrastructures ranging from cloud hosts to interface design [@donovanNavigatingTechStack2019]. Each of these spaces has complementary speech affordances in policies and enforcement mechanisms, and utilize fragmentation as a prop for competition in a market where platforms distinguish themselves by different moderation philosophies. 

Despite their fragmentation, what binds these alternative spaces conceptually? These tectonic shifts have been described once before in the political and cultural arena, sometimes as opposition or alternative parties [@downsCordonSanitaireEffective2012], and other times as subcultures countering a real or perceived “mainstream” [@oharaFourInternetsData2021]. Depending on the point of view, the “alternativity” may be welcomed by some as a source of cultural transformation, and by others regarded as an existential threat to a normative order. A more descriptive, common denominator is the idea of “counter-spheres” emerging from fundamental ruptures in consensus about speech norms — that is, over what can and cannot be said in public and why. Departing from the premise that speech norms are “essentially contested” [@delaatCoercionEmpowermentModeration2012] and subject to constant “discursive contestation” [@fraserRethinkingPublicSphere1990], alternative platforms constitute themselves as normative and technical alternatives to unresolved disagreements, building complementary affordances to bypass the limitations of their former hosts.

To illustrate this process, this chapter takes a distant reading approach to reconstruct a brief history of major normative ruptures that have given rise to alt-tech spheres across the Web, including cloud hosts, the blogging sphere, wikis, social media, messaging applications, and LLMs. It then compares the normative and technical affordances of selected social media spaces — namely Twitter, X and Truth Social. In doing so, it highlights how these spaces, despite their competition, coexist and complement one another’s normative and technical limitations. I conclude with considerations on the importance of consensus-building mechanisms around content moderation and speech norms, given that platforms still rely on their legitimacy as (nominal) spaces for public debate. 

## **The infrastructure of speech affordances**

Before we explore the formation and constitution of alt-tech, it may be useful to revisit what the literature has meant by “alt-tech” — particularly since their formation has been mired in speech controversies. 

One way that the literature has answered the question is through speech. Zuckerman and Rajendra-Nicolucci use *alt-tech* for platforms offering a “promise of uncensored speech” to publics united by the grievance that their speech has been censored for failing to be politically correct [@zuckermanDeplatformingOurWay2021]. The alternativity in alt-tech, in this account, is first of all normative: a platform presents itself as the place where something that could not be said elsewhere may be said again. A complementary answer is thus about *speech affordances*. Rogers describes alternative platforms as destinations to which “deplatformed social media celebrities migrate”, often because they provide communicative possibilities that their former hosts do not [@rogersDeplatformingFollowingExtreme2020]. Telegram’s appeal, for example, lies partly in a more private sociality: channels, groups and messaging allow an actor to retain an audience while withdrawing contentious material from the more visible and actively moderated surfaces of Facebook, Twitter or YouTube. In this sense, an alternative platform is not only a refuge for particular speech. It is an arrangement of possibilities for publishing, addressing, finding and circulating it.

Donovan, Lewis and Friedberg’s earlier metaphor of “parallel ports” joins these two definitions by treating alt-tech primarily as an infrastructural project [@donovanParallelPortsSociotechnical2019]. A parallel port was an early hardware solution for connecting peripherals through which several streams of data could travel simultaneously. Applied to alt-tech, the metaphor does not describe a sealed counter-world, but a supplementary route through which networked movements preserve and move what other platforms will not host. It is useful precisely because these movements are at once structured by technical infrastructure and involved in structuring infrastructures of their own. Hosting, domain registration, payment systems, databases, interfaces and moderation mechanisms therefore belong to the same speech arrangement, where each can become a point at which expression is enabled, marginalized or cut off.

Later accounts have tended to locate alt-tech’s *alternativity* within this infrastructural arrangement. Siapera, for example, describes BitChute as a political media infrastructure sustaining political microcelebrities and “ideology entrepreneurs”, with business models, relations to user data and ideals of audience self-reliance that depart from those of dominant platforms [@siaperaAltTechPublic2023b, pp. 447–448, 463]. Otero and Scharlach similarly show that alt-tech safety is not organized by an absence of moderation, but by a rival conception of what safety is, whom it protects and how far a platform should “marshal” what users encounter [@oteroWeNotMarshal2025]. Platforms compete over audiences and technical features, but also over moderation philosophies: over which harms matter, which authorities may define them and what degree of intervention speech should encounter.

The technical differences do, however, reveal a far more interdependent relation with mainstream competitors. From an infrastructural viewpoint, for example, Truth Social reproduces the profile, feed, reply and repost conventions of Twitter with relatively little change to their communicative grammar; its own open-source page identifies Mastodon among the software on which it was built [@TrumpsNewSocial2021]. What changes more decisively is the normative orientation attached to them: Truth Social’s community guidelines explicitly permit trolling within a non-partisan “Big Tent” of ideas (Truth Social, 2022), where Twitter’s policies had named hateful conduct and abuse as distinct objects of enforcement. Twitter and Truth Social, and later Twitter and X, are thus best understood not as technological opposites but as normatively complementary infrastructures, where each makes a market of what the other is accused of excluding.

## **Alt-tech as counter-spheres**

Fraser’s notion of counterpublics provides a useful theoretical framework with which to think of these complementary relations. Counterpublics are “parallel discursive arenas” in which subordinated groups invent and circulate counter-discourses, and through them formulate oppositional accounts of their identities, interests and needs [@fraserRethinkingPublicSphere1990]. I use *counter-sphere*, here, to widen this description from a public and its discourse to the environment that helps make both possible: the norms, cultures, markets and technical infrastructures through which a counter-discourse can be produced, preserved and circulated. This is not meant to recover alt-tech as a new version of the virtuous countercultures of the 1960s; they may and do practice their own exclusions and forms of marginalization, accepting some expressions and not others [@fraserRethinkingPublicSphere1990]. But this exclusion remains complementary, in the sense that counter-spheres define themselves and permit what they host against the limitations of a competing sphere. 

How, more precisely, does a platform acquire this relation? As alluded to in [Part II. Speech affordances](#/part-2), public spheres, and platforms especially, are plurivocal environments constituted by speech norms: conventions and other rules that arbitrate what can be said, in what manner and to whom. In this context, moderation is the speech management mechanism through which platforms operationalize those norms in policies and techniques, codes of conduct, interface features and wider speech cultures. These mechanisms produce *speech affordances*, conceived within different thresholds of tolerance. Different platforms will decide how and when to enforce their boundary based on different normative orientations, using demotion, obfuscation, labels or temporary suspensions to marginalize objectionable speech until it reaches a given threshold. 

These boundaries are neither self-evident nor still. Speech norms are “essentially contested” [@delaatCoercionEmpowermentModeration2012], and their meaning remains subject to the “discursive contestation” through which publics negotiate the terms of their own debate [@fraserRethinkingPublicSphere1990]. What counts as problematic speech consequently depends less on a quality intrinsic to the utterance than on how hard it bumps against the dynamic “boundaries of acceptable norms of public culture” [@pohjonenExtremeSpeechOnline]. The same statement may be tolerated, contextualized, marginalized or removed as it passes from Twitter to Truth Social, or from Twitter into X. Each response positions the statement along a different threshold of tolerance; each gives a different material answer to what counts as acceptable and why.

Most such disagreement is absorbed by the ordinary negotiation of speech norms. A counter-sphere begins to form when it is not. When users and platforms no longer share the definitions, authorities or procedures by which a disputed utterance might be judged, a moderation decision becomes a rupture. The platform may excommunicate the content or user; the user may leave before it does; an infrastructural provider may withdraw the means by which the speech is hosted at all. In each case, what was excluded can become the organizing matter of another space, whose first affordance is to permit what the former one would not. Alt-tech platforms are counter-spheres in this precise sense: not because they abolish moderation, but because they materialize an alternative settlement of a disagreement that could no longer be contained within the same sphere.

# **The formation of alt-tech counter-spheres**

Alt-tech platforms tend to emerge from epistemic or normative ruptures with their host environments. Continuous deliberation over speech norms can culminate in disagreements so deep that they become intractable. Such moments may lead to self-exile, when users voluntarily depart, or to forced exile, when users are deplatformed. In either case, the rupture becomes the generative event for alternative spaces. What distinguishes these ruptures is not merely their procedural form, but the fact that disagreement cannot be absorbed or resolved within the platform’s prevailing order of norms. They mark points at which the public sphere of a given platform becomes unsustainable for certain groups, creating the demand for competing or parallel arenas.

This dynamic can be conceptualized as a “platform rupture”: a moment in which content that is not necessarily illegal nonetheless becomes the subject of profound moral or intellectual disagreement with platform norms, whether as defined by policies or as interpreted by users. While the notion resembles “deplatforming”, the emphasis here is on the quality of intractability. Deplatforming refers to processes of transgression and destitution: users and their content are removed for violating rules, and in the process they lose access to audiences, visibility, and circulation channels [@rogersDeplatformingFollowingExtreme2020]. A rupture, however, describes a severance grounded in deep cultural or epistemic disagreement. It may result from a ban, but it may also take the form of voluntary departure, as with academic and left-leaning communities leaving X for Mastodon or Bluesky [@lehrWhitherPublicInternet2019; @oharaFourInternetsData2021]. The phenomenon can also be infrastructural, as when a cloud service refuses to host controversial content, forcing the creation of alternatives.

Rupture is typically followed by exile, whether voluntary or imposed. Exiled communities often attempt to recreate the functions of the platforms they left, while reconfiguring them around different speech norms. For instance, when contributors are banned from Wikipedia, they may fork the database to create alternative wikis that preserve or amend contested versions of content. Yet even within these forks, disagreements frequently persist, generating further splits and new competing projects. In this sense, exile is rarely the endpoint. Instead, it sets in motion cycles of fragmentation in which alternative spaces reproduce both the contentions and the affordances of their predecessors, thereby reinforcing the pattern of hyperfragmentation described by Siapera [-@siaperaAltTechPublic2023b].

## **Alt-tech, then and again: a timeline**

Taking a broader perspective, it becomes clear that alt-tech spaces have arisen across many domains of the Web. These include fora and news aggregators, domain registration and cloud services, search engines, messengers, blogs, social media platforms, wikis, and video streaming services. In each of these areas we find competitors (represented by lighter streams in the timeline below) as well as _normative alternatives_ (represented by darker streams). Some alternatives emerged out of disagreements over privacy, as in the cases of Telegram, Signal, DuckDuckGo or MeWe. Others crystallized around conflicts over moderation norms. Still others developed out of geopolitical ruptures, as in the cases of Rutube, Yandex or Ruwiki.

![](images/E%20de%20Keulenaar%20-%20Online%20deviance%202024%20timeline%20of%20social%20media%20history-01.png)

Within the video-streaming sphere, BitChute was founded in 2017 during a wave of YouTube deplatforming that affected far-right actors in Europe and the United States [@douekMoreContentModeration2021]. Rumble, established earlier in 2013, also gained new audiences during this period of migration. In the social media sphere, a cascade of alternatives emerged in response to exclusions from Facebook and Twitter. Minds was created in 2015 with an emphasis on privacy [@oharaFourInternetsData2021]. Gab appeared in 2016, partly in reaction to bans of alt-right figures [@balkinFreeSpeechTriangle2018]. Parler launched in 2018, while GETTR and Truth Social were introduced in 2021 following the deplatforming of Donald Trump and other actors implicated in the Stop the Steal campaign and the Capitol Hill riots. X, which replaced Twitter in 2023, absorbed users who had migrated to Gab, Parler, GETTR and Truth Social, while at the same time prompting others to move to Mastodon. These sequences illustrate how platform ruptures generate waves of exit and re-entry, producing a constantly refracted ecosystem.

![](images/E%20de%20Keulenaar%20-%20Alt%20tech%20timelines%20v.%202.png)

Sometimes, users migrated away from microblogging services altogether, favoring the private and encrypted affordances of messengers such as Telegram or Signal. In the domain of bulletin boards and registrars, similar cycles can be observed. Even in spaces that prided themselves on minimal moderation, disagreements and migrations occurred. Users left 4chan for 8chan in 2013, only to move again to 8kun in 2019 after further controversies. The latter replatforming was made possible when alternative providers such as Epik and the Russian company DDoS-Guard offered services that Cloudflare refused [@balkinFreeSpeechTriangle2018]. Similarly, Telegram has served as a refuge for numerous deplatformed celebrities [@rogersDeplatformingFollowingExtreme2020]. These infrastructural supports illustrate how moderation ruptures often overlap with geopolitical and economic dimensions, producing synergies that sustain the proliferation of alt-tech counter-spheres.

## **The interdependence of mainstream and alt-tech**

As recalled in [Chapter 9. Normative dislocation](#/chapter-9), Telegram, often presented as a space beyond the reach of Western regulators and platform moderation regimes, has functioned as a hub for planning, coordination and discussion. In the case of the Brazilian riots, users relied on Telegram to organize events in extreme detail, circumventing oversight by state authorities and corporate actors. Yet at the same time, coded or strategically fragmented messages were released on mainstream platforms — Facebook, Instagram, Twitter — in order to reach larger audiences, without provoking immediate interventions. In this way, the original and more contentious content remained preserved and accessible on Telegram, while fragments disseminated elsewhere scaffolded the meaning in ways that deferred or displaced controversy.

This dynamic produces a form of cross-platform interdependence where alternative and mainstream platforms stand as infrastructures that sustain each other’s circulation of moderated content. The mainstream becomes a distribution channel, extending the reach of the alternative. From Twitter, Facebook and YouTube, users circulated links to GETTR, Telegram, CloutHub, Odysee, Rumble, BitChute, Infowars and the website brazilwasstolen.com, which hosted documents that had been removed by Brazil’s Supreme Federal Court for disinformation, but which remained accessible through VPNs. Alternative spaces may depend on the mainstream for visibility, while mainstream actors themselves cannot fully disentangle from what they have excluded.

## **Platforms, from frontiers to contested borders**

This brings into question the nature of *frontiers* in a platform. Platforms are commonly depicted as relatively limitless spaces, with “frontier” meaning “front”, the “foreland”, the “margins” — a space of expansion where boundaries are not yet fully consolidated. Frontiers are directional, suggesting movement toward new territory, the expansion of platformization and the attraction of new audiences [@kristofNatureFrontiersBoundaries1959, pp. 269–270]. They are the outer edges of spaces that grow by extending themselves further, much like the Westward expansion of states or empires.

Moderation policies, as alluded to in [Chapter 6. Moderation in crisis](#/chapter-6), would tend to transform frontiers into borders. Enforced rules and techniques act as stricter delimiters, marking the platform’s extent and internal identity; they enact what scholars call “boundary work” [@matiasCivicLaborVolunteer2019, pp. 2–4]: an “inner-oriented” demarcation that affirms the cohesion of the platform’s normative order [@kristofNatureFrontiersBoundaries1959, p. 272]. At the core of these boundaries is a “jural law” acting as a “formal verbalization and particularization of the moral standards of a given socio-political order” [@kristofNatureFrontiersBoundaries1959, p. 257]. From it, moderation determines the normative possibilities of speech, and thus of what circulates within. 

With increased enforcement, however, borders (ironically) become contested “points of contact” from which content and users transgress and return across a network of more or less moderated spaces. In other words, enforcement generates further exchange [@sarafFrontiers2020, p. 1], moved by normative tensions. Policy boundaries hold coherence internally, but at the edges — particularly where they are the object of fundamental disagreement — they become “spaces of indeterminacy and volatility” [@sarafFrontiers2020] that end up forming newer, counter, or competing territories. This also applies internally: Twitter, for example, remodeled itself in the image of that which had once been excluded.

## **Conclusion**

This chapter asked what binds alt-tech spaces conceptually and how they emerge from the platformized Web. The literature has generally answered the first question by describing what makes these spaces alternative: their subcultures and promises of uncensored expression [@zuckermanDeplatformingOurWay2021], their communicative affordances and their attraction to deplatformed users [@rogersDeplatformingFollowingExtreme2020], or the parallel infrastructures that allow excluded content to remain online [@donovanNavigatingTechStack2019]. These accounts identify important features of alt-tech, but they tend to begin after the alternative has already formed.

The argument made here begins with the relation that produces it. Alt-tech spaces are counter-spheres formed through ruptures in consensus over speech norms. Such norms are essentially contested: disagreement is not an incidental failure in their application, but part of the process through which their meaning is made and revised [@delaatCoercionEmpowermentModeration2012; @fraserRethinkingPublicSphere1990]. A platform rupture occurs when that contestation can no longer be contained within the same normative and technical environment. Users may be removed, leave voluntarily or lose access to an infrastructural provider. In each case, the content at issue and the disagreement surrounding it become material for another space.

This places the present fragmentation within a longer history of the Web. Sunstein described an early movement away from common information sources toward fragmented clusters of attention [@sunsteinRepubliccom2002]. Platformization appeared to reverse that tendency by consolidating activity around a small number of intermediaries and extending their mechanisms into other social domains [@helmondPlatformizationWebMaking2015; @vandijckPlatformMechanisms2018]. Alt-tech introduces another form of fragmentation within this consolidated environment. It does not simply multiply sources of information. It produces platforms differentiated by the speech norms they claim to protect, the content they permit and the moderation philosophies through which they compete.

The history reconstructed in this chapter shows that this process is not confined to social media. Normative alternatives have formed across cloud services, domain registration, bulletin boards, wikis, messengers, search, blogging and video hosting. Nor do these alternatives abolish moderation. As Siapera argues, alt-tech is better understood as *competing moderation*: infrastructures organized around different judgments about what needs protection, what constitutes excess and who should decide [@siaperaAltTechPublic2023b, p. 456]. The reproduction of familiar interfaces and platform functions makes this especially clear. Alternatives often duplicate the technical affordances of the spaces from which they break while changing the normative boundaries that govern them.

The concept of the counter-sphere clarifies both the political force and the limits of this arrangement. Counter-spheres allow excluded publics to circulate interpretations, identities and claims that their former hosts do not accommodate [@fraserRethinkingPublicSphere1990]. They should not, however, be treated as necessarily emancipatory. Counterpublics can reproduce exclusions of their own, including anti-democratic and anti-egalitarian ones. What makes alt-tech counter-spheric is therefore not the virtue of its speech, but its relational position: it constitutes itself against a mainstream whose rules it experiences as limiting, coercive or illegitimate.

That relation continues after rupture. Donovan’s metaphor of “parallel ports” captures the infrastructural complementarity through which removed content circulates alongside mainstream platforms [@donovanNavigatingTechStack2019]. Rogers similarly shows that deplatformed actors may adopt the more permissive or private affordances of alternative services without surrendering their dependence on mainstream visibility [@rogersDeplatformingFollowingExtreme2020]. The cases examined here develop this point. Alternative spaces preserve, coordinate or elaborate contentious material; mainstream platforms provide access to larger audiences and wider circuits of attention. The two do not form sealed media systems. They become interdependent parts of the same ecology.

This is why the distinction between frontiers and borders matters. Platformization is often narrated through the frontier: an outward movement into spaces whose limits have yet to be fixed. Moderation turns those frontiers into borders by formalizing and enforcing the normative order of a platform [@kristofNatureFrontiersBoundaries1959, p. 257; @matiasCivicLaborVolunteer2019, pp. 2–4]. Yet borders are also points of contact. The material they exclude can return through links, fragments, screenshots, mirrors and newly built infrastructures. At the point of normative disagreement, the border becomes a space of volatility from which further territories may form [@sarafFrontiers2020, p. 1]. Rupture is therefore not only an effect of a platform boundary. It is one of the ways in which that boundary becomes productive.

The longer-term fate of any particular alt-tech platform remains uncertain. Some lose resources, users and relevance; others persist at a smaller scale; still others see their norms or users return to mainstream spaces. But platform survival is not the only measure of consequence. A small alternative can continue to host material, sustain an interpretive community or provide references that circulate elsewhere. Likewise, normalization does not require an alternative platform to defeat its competitor. Its speech norms may move back into the mainstream, as the transformation of Twitter into X demonstrates, changing the center through a rupture that initially appeared to concern its margins.

Deplatforming should therefore be understood as a reorganization of speech rather than its disappearance. It can reduce reach in a particular space and deprive actors of specific affordances [@rogersDeplatformingFollowingExtreme2020]. It may also produce the exile through which alternative infrastructures and identities consolidate. The point is not that exclusion has no effect, but that its effects extend beyond the platform enforcing it. When a disagreement over speech norms cannot be worked through, moderation redistributes content, users and authority across a wider platform ecology.

The implication is not that every form of contested speech must remain unrestricted within every public sphere. It is that public spheres also require places in which difficult disagreements over their limits can be made explicit. This is the function of *parrhesia* in the argument developed here: not an absence of rules, but an affordance for speaking about what the rules exclude and why. Where such disagreement has no common venue, it is more likely to be displaced into counter-spheres whose identity depends on the rupture. Consensus over speech norms will remain incomplete and revisable. The more sustainable task is therefore to provide forms of metamoderation through which platforms and their publics can contest those norms before enforcement becomes their only point of contact.
