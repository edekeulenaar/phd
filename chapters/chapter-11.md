# Chapter 11. Epistemic ruptures in the encyclopedias of alternative facts

In what may be regarded as the founding text of postmodern theory, Jean-François Lyotard posited that epistemics would become detached from the great “metanarratives” that had heretofore organized modern Western thinking [@lyotardConditionPostmoderneRapport2009]. What is less well appreciated about his influential contribution is the advent of computation as motivating this paradigm shift [@lyotardConditionPostmoderneRapport2009, 12]. Computation would mean that knowledge is processed as complex systems of information that the individual might then collect, organise and repurpose at will [@lyotardConditionPostmoderneRapport2009, xviii]. While ‘modern’ epistemics promised total (and totalitarian) interpretations of the world from above, the “postmodern condition” would facilitate a new epistemic condition based on redefining knowledge as information devoid of metanarrative qualities.

Lyotard’s framing may retrospectively be seen to resonate with evocations of the web and social media platforms as ‘open’ and democratic spaces. This perspective, sometimes referred to as "peer-production consensus", has often taken as one of is central case studies the Wikipedia project [@kreissLimitsPeerProduction2011]. The epistemics of Wikipedia promises total access to an authoritative and ever growing inventory of knowledge. This feat is accomplished through a laudable technical infrastructure that seeks to accommodate diverging interpretations while at the same taking cautions against "bias" as ideological or personal preferences [@wikipediaWikipediaFringeTheories2018]. In dialogue with Lyotard’s theory of postmodern epistemics, we argue that the presence of alternative encyclopaedias, or what we call *altpedias*, reveal the limitations of the epistemic project behind Wikipedia as an example of a mainstream platform. 

As illustrated in [Chapter 10. Platform ruptures and the formation of online counter spheres](#/chapter-10), the tale of this rupture is technically similar to that of other platforms. Entries that do not conform to Wikipedia’s neutral point of view policy[^2] often migrate to altpedias initiated by editors banned from having posted content that violated Wikipedia's editorial policies. As a result, altpedia entries attempt to bring light to hidden and censored knowledge. They are "partisan" in the sense that their antagonistic framing of encyclopaedic knowledge undermines the premise that there can be any neutral consensus on universal knowledge production. Rather, knowledge is a normative good entwined within political projects engaged in counter-hegemonic struggles against a liberal mainstream. 

Historically, this rupture already follows from long-standing critiques of liberalism. Deneen would describe it as a political *ecosystem* that, "like water for a fish", surrounds us without being perceived [@deneenWhyLiberalismFailed2018, 4]. And while its "pretense to neutrality" [@deneenWhyLiberalismFailed2018, 5] may be the product of peer production, as intended by consensus-building processes, it meets the edge of its world wherever there are deep disagreements [@fogelinLogicDeepDisagreements1985]. And while these disagreement may not always be unresolvable, the presumption that they are marginal, fringe and don't merit attention contributes to the criticism of liberalism as a hegemonic ideology. 

The small but growing world of alternative platforms and wikis — of which the latest instalment includes Grokipedia — makes a similar criticism when it comes to mainstream platforms. But while they seem to advocate for radical standpoints, they maintain the same ambition as their mainstream counterparts to universalise their epistemic norms through a platform logic. They first serve as repositories of otherwise rejected knowledge, and eventually engage in Hegelian "struggle to the death" [@hegelGeorgWilhelmFriedrich2018] for engagement and influence on an aspirationally platformized scale. And while they use the very same software, they in fact platformize a project of ideological reform. That is: they go on to edit forked content as knowledge reformed under their ideological precepts, as a direct editorial response to Wikipedia. 

Thus, the epistemic guidelines they lay regarding what is true, correct or objective tends to associate values of objectivity to justice, particularly as a retribution for their rejection. This key feature is at the foundation of what we refer as platform ruptures. It is in an effort to elaborate on these ruptures that we will shortly proceed to examine altpedias as examples of this broader process. Before we proceed with an empirical study, it is however necessary to situate altpedias in relation to the broaden and essentially political crisis that characterised “post-truth epistemics” online.

### Post-truth as partisan epistemics

A political autopsy genre has emerged in the wake of the right-wing populist insurgencies of the mid-2010s according to which the crisis in liberal democracies is a crisis of epistemology where truth and facts are losing their objective status [@kakutaniDeathTruthHow2018; @mcintyrePostTruth2018]. One of the favourite culprits of this problem has been postmodernism. The argument often presented is that postmodernism is guilty of epistemological relativism, initially as a hermeneutic method, and eventually as a destabiliser of knowledge production of Western liberal societies. What began as an alternative way to read novels transformed into a method for feminists to critique scientific hubris, which then became a way for creationists and climate change skeptics to deny science, which in turn opened the door to fake news. Humanities scholars figure like a proverbial Dr. Frankenstein creating a monster whose power failed to comprehend or adequately care for the fragile conditions of knowledge. 

While postmodern theory may have been applied programmatically by the acolytes of so-called 'French Theory' within Anglo-American academia [@cussetFrenchTheoryHow2008], in its initial formulation, the notion of a *postmodern condition* was not intended as a normative prescription. It referred instead to a speculative diagnosis concerning the potential effects of computation on epistemology. It is thus in regards to this original diagnosis, which can be understood as having effectively inaugurated the whole discourse on postmodernism in Anglo-American cultural theory, that we would like to position our approach in the study below. 

In 1979, Jean-Francois Lyotard inaugurated a new intellectual paradigm within critical theory with his famous characterization of what he called “la condition postmoderne” (the postmodern condition). According to his periodization argument, the “redeployment of advanced liberal capitalism” and the “blossoming of techniques and technologies” have “shifted the emphasis from the ends of actions to [their] means” since approximately the mid-century [@lyotardPostmodernConditionReport1984, 37]. The result of this paradigm shift was that “metanarratives” that stemmed from the nineteenth-century German model of knowledge, which married narrative to scientific knowledge (or education of the Spirit with that of Reason), lost their legitimacy in this new epistemic condition [@lyotardPostmodernConditionReport1984, 32-37].

In place of narrative guidelines, computerisation becomes the organizing principle of postmodern epistemics. The consequence of this shift was for knowledge to be redefined as information geared towards what Lyotard referred to as “performativity” [@lyotardPostmodernConditionReport1984, 11]. Computerised knowledge abandoned a unitary and totalizing epistemics [@lyotardPostmodernConditionReport1984, 11-12] in favour of a “principle of a plurality of formal and axiomatic systems capable of arguing the truth of denotative statements” [@lyotardPostmodernConditionReport1984, 43-53]. Still, the idea of universality as associated with former modernist metanarratives was not altogether abandoned, as it could be seen to persist in the instrumental aim of computation to organise, aggregate, link and access data as a "totalising" enterprise [@grattonJeanFrancoisLyotard2018], where "data banks are the encyclopedia of tomorrow" [@lyotardPostmodernConditionReport1984, 43]. This critique begins to echo one often made against platforms as a totalising project to aggregate and reorganise all data as a service for total access to knowledge and services [@diamondRoadDigitalUnfreedom2019], with dire consequences on individual privacy and epistemic diversity ("paralogy", in Lyotard's terms). 

Lyotard’s notion of postmodern epistemics offers insights into the current multiplication of claims to truth online, for example in terms of "fake news" and "post-truth". While the latter problem is typically framed in terms of malicious "manipulation" by ill-intentioned actors [@MediaManipulationDisinformation2017], we may also perceive some of these phenomena in terms of a broader epistemic struggle against the neutralization of the “paralogous” quality of knowledge into a single coherent, consensus-based account [@lyotardPostmodernConditionReport1984, 60]. While the postmodern dimension of the Wikipedia project has been the subject of studies from a great many perspectives [@rodriguezLiberatingEpistemologyWikipedia2007; @robertsonPostmodernShiftLibrary2013a, 11; @leshnickTechnologyMeetsPostmodern2016a], Lyotard arguably offers a relatively unique perspective from which to critique the former as an essentially liberal technology of epistemological power. From his agonistic perspective, it can be argued that Wikipedia effectively mollifies ideological dissent by subjecting knowledge to the liberal principle of consensus and neutrality. 

In recent years, Wikipedia has gained competition from a number of alternative encyclopedic wikis that make use of the same underlying MediaWiki software as Wikipedia, but governed according to different criteria. These wikis challenge what they perceive to be the liberal and neutral penchants of the majority of Wikipedia editors, often doing so in explicitly antagonistic political terms [@johnsonRightwingWebsiteChallenges2007a; @rooseAltRightCreatedParallel2017; @sobelfittsWelcomeWikipediaAltRight2017]. These alternative wikis are not limited to the following, but for the purposes of this study we will consider Metapedia, a self-described pro-European encyclopedia espousing Holocaust denialist viewpoints; Infogalactic, a wiki created by “alt-right” activist Vox Day; and Rightpedia, a wiki devoted primarily to extreme-right conspiracy theory.[^4] 

If we consider Wikipedia to be an example of non-narrative-based postmodern knowledge, then the presence of marginal agonistic alterpedias may be understood as emphasizing Lyotard’s broader argument that the postmodern condition is above all characterized by questions of universal truth being displaced by competing language games — regardless of what those involved may or may not believe to be true. This premise has been proposed by Farkas and Schou (2018), who examine the notion of fake news as technique to radically challenge and eventually recast epistemic hegemonies [@farkasFakeNewsFloating2018, 302]. One could say that the disqualification of information as "fake" reveals the underlying “agonistics” of all forms of political discourse in which "to speak is to fight" [@lyotardConditionPostmoderneRapport2009, 10]. In refusing to compromise with Wikipedia, altpedia editors seek to curate their territories on their own fundamentally agonistics terms.[^5] 

Constituting many tens of thousands of topics each, these projects, particularly Infogalactic and Metapedia, have largely been created by dissenting Wikipedia editors wanting to publish information banned by other editors in Wikipedia for transgressing its ethical codes, particularly those sanctioning racist and pseudoscientific content. Whilst on average they only have a few dozen active contributors [@infogalacticInfogalacticIntroduction2018; @metapediaMetapediaCounteringSemantic2021], partisan altpedias constitute in themselves entire universes of alternative facts that stand in (perceived) competition to the epistemic ambitions of Wikipedia — this,  despite its resilient dispute resolution protocols. Altpedia editors regard their wikis as both universal repositories of taboo knowledge *and* places from which to make that type of knowledge universally plausible and accessible in the authoritative form of an encyclopedia. However marginal these projects may be in comparison to Wikipedia, they are worthy of study as examples of *platform ruptures*, extending their alternative historical, political and social interpretations of world history into separate consensus building projects. 

## Mapping the partisan epistemics of altpedias

Before we can proceed with our political analysis of these spaces, we must begin by first outlining our empirical method. Our approach was divided into two steps, each with different objectives. The aim of the first of these was to gain a perspective as to why and how altpedias branched off of Wikipedia — what we call above *platform ruptures*. The aim of second was to see how each wiki went on to build their own compedia of partisan knowledge, to investigate how they justify their role as repositories of knowledge (or, as online encyclopaedias), what purposes they find in being one, and how the normative dimension of their mission as encyclopaedias affects their conception of knowledge. 

To examine platform ruptures, we traced the history of one specific article on Race and Intelligence from Wikipedia all the way through its respective forks on Metapedia, Rightpedia, and Infogalactic.[^6] We chose this page because, besides being relatively popular in all altpedias, it was the central point of contention in Wikipedia from which altpedias were created. The page's history on Wikipedia thus contains important metadata of editor disputes, or so-called “edit wars” leading to migrations into altpedias. These edit wars are central pieces of deep disagreement [@fogelinLogicDeepDisagreements1985] constituting platform rupture. 

Through the APIs of Wikipedia and Metapedia, we were able to extract all text from the relevant pages, as well as their revision histories and size. Combined with a manual analysis of the page’s history on other wikis, edit history, size and textual content, we traced page edits and their sizes. We then discovered that Wikipedia was the first to publish a page on the subject, and that, since then, only Metapedia and Infogalactic forked from it at various points until Metapedia’s version was forked to Rightpedia.

Significant editing moments were determined by a combination of edit size and important changes indicated by the history of a page’s table of content.

Our second step consisted in examining how each altpedia distinguished itself in epistemic terms. To do so, we focussed our analysis on altpedias’ “meta” pages, where they specify their mission statements, their reference page (‘About’), and their content guideline pages. We identified these internal pages by their title, which would usually juxtapose the name of the wiki and the subject of each of these pages (for example, ‘Wikipedia:Manual of Style’). Seeing as these pages were too numerous to be studied through a qualitative close reading, we manually extracted key terms that refer to what content each wiki wants and does not want. Such terms would then range from concepts (‘veracity’, ‘neutrality’, ‘clarity’) to specific forms (‘cited references’, ‘clear sentences’) and conducts (‘good faith editing’, ‘being nice’). To compare these terms across wikis, we made networks formed by connections between wikis having such concepts in common. 

After having scraped the altpedias, we aimed to gain a better grasp of the meaning of key concepts expressed in mission statements and content guidelines. In spite of their opposition to Wikipedia, we found altpedias also to paradoxically make use the concept of neutrality in their guidelines, as well as veracity, balance, and other editorial norms. The question was how these norms were operationalised. We extracted the text from these pages and trained separate word2vec word embedding models that locate words in a vector space, thereby representing these words’ local contexts of use — such that proximate words can be understood as close in meaning [@mikolovComputingNumericRepresentations2015]. We then plotted the frequency count of words extracted from the content guidelines of each wiki using the scatter text term frequency algorithm [@kesslerScattertext2019]. Finally, to capture the essence of contention between and within each Altpedia, we manually identified and compared terms each wiki uses to refer to one another (see **Figure 8**). 

## Altpedia edit wars as epistemic ruptures

In many respects, Wikipedia responds to the new epistemological parameters Lyotard outlines as part of postmodern societies. Since its inception in 2002, Wikipedia was designed to afford knowledge in all its diversity (be it in terms of information, sources, or historical and ideological perspectives) [@wikipediaWikipedia2018; @wikipediaWikipediaPurpose2018]. It attempts to place users beyond dissent and irreconcilable points of views, particularly by way of affordances and written norms, conduct and content guidelines encouraging users to engage in dialogue and reach consensus about the content they publish and edit. Users usually convene in talk pages, where they discuss how and whether content attends to the rules and norms that the Wikipedia content guidelines outline, such as veracity, relevance, accuracy and neutrality. Rules of conduct such as good faith editing, as well as a designated hierarchy between a handful of levels of editors, guarantee that these ‘general agonistics’, in Lyotard's formulation, do not descend into the total antagonism of ‘edit wars’ wherein users fundamentally disagree and ceaselessly edit each other’s edits. These rules designate both practical and epistemological affordances that delimit the criteria under which information is ‘good’, which edits are approved or disproved, which are rejected, and how and why editors are banned.

Despite its robust affordances, the very existence of altpedias reveal that Wikipedia does not enjoy universal consensus over its epistemological legitimacy and moral authority. The presence of multiple wikis branching off of Wikipedia, each with their different idea as to what true and just knowledge is, reflects how different conceptions of knowledge are operationalised into different spaces. Content guidelines — which for example ban pseudoscientific content, misinformation, conspiracies and caution with fringe theories [@wikipediaWikipediaFringeTheories2018] — as well as its rules of conduct provide the basis for contributions to be rejected and account to be banned. Once banned, contributors fork onto alternative wikis. Edit wars may however continue, leading to further forking (even in altpedias) (**Figure 4**).

We call this process a "platform rupture". After proposing that his or her contributions be labelled as "alternative opinions" rather than false claims, the editor in question is turned down and goes on to recast his or her preferred version of the Wikipedia article into Infogalactic, where they and other editors rewrite the page overtime.

While the epistemic language of their metapages may make altpedias appear strikingly similar to Wikipedia, topics worthy of inclusion are generally those that have been explicitly excluded from Wikipedia, for instance Holocaust revisionism, the correlation between race and IQ, or white supremacy, as if by rescuing these from their general classification as objects of poor or gravely immoral knowledge.[^8] This tightly knit relation between knowledge, authority and justice echoes Lyotard’s own description of what he called the ‘delegitimation’ of competing types of knowledge [@lyotardPostmodernConditionReport1984, 37]. At the source of the questioned legitimacy of a type of knowledge is a question of ‘double legitimation’: ‘\[…\] who decides what knowledge is, and who knows what needs to be decided?’ [@lyotardPostmodernConditionReport1984, 8-9]. Both of these questions are answered by each altpedia with knowledge legitimised by alternative norms, each of which begin at the end of post-ideological liberal visions of knowledge. 

## The future of weaponized knowledge? 

As discussed at the outset, a post-truth autopsy genre has sought to pin blame on postmodern theory for having induced the current crisis in liberal democracies, finding the latter responsible for having infected epistemology with a fatal strain of relativism. Our case study of altpedias, as exemplary sites for the production of alternative facts, suggests however that the philosophical premises of these arguments could be revised. It is not that partisan projects like altpedias actively seek to undermine a consensus version of scientifically valid information as developed within modern Western liberal societies, nor for that matter that their criteria of truth are necessarily distinct from the latter. Altpedias are rather manifestations of worldviews expressed through "language games" that attempt to do justice to information they perceive as not being known enough, thereby re-establishing their own criteria as to what is and is not worth knowing. They can be taken as an examples of a political instrumentalization of facts that is not just marked by disagreements over content, but also over concerns about what qualifies as worthy of being known, and thus knowable, by virtue of their partisan premises.  

Lyotard was perceptive in speculating on "\[d\]ata banks \[as\] the encyclopedia of tomorrow" [@lyotardPostmodernConditionReport1984, 43]. But while Wikipedia may indeed represent the "principle of a plurality of formal and axiomatic systems capable of arguing the truth of denotative statements" [@lyotardPostmodernConditionReport1984, 53], in their "paralogical" definition of multiple worlds of truth [@lyotardPostmodernConditionReport1984, 60], altpedias in a sense represent more than postmodern encyclopaedias. As is the case in altpedias, normative definitions of knowledge are still elucidated by different readings of history, and, in this sense, of narrative knowledge. While Wikipedia has been designed to host as many "narratives" as possible, the argument posted by altpedias is that it does so within a limited spectrum governed by the normative criteria of liberal consensus, which seeks to dampen the essentially contested nature of epistemics. Wikipedia is cast as an example of a hegemonic representative of false neutrality, opposed by altpedias in a zero-sum game of *weaponized knowledge production*. 

And so, while they may indeed be used as resources to manufacture all manner of misinformation, it is inaccurate to conceptualize this problematic in terms of post-truth. Altpedias are not so much a sign of the obsolescence of truth, but rather evidence that compendia of knowledge designed to harmonise all knowledge and withstand epistemic ruptures are still delimited in their conceptions as to what acceptable knowledge is. Beyond Wikipedia lie the altpedias, which seek to imbue their partisan politics with the universal tone, format and functions of the utopian project of open source knowledge, and to provide refuge for those forms of knowledge excluded from the epistemic mainstream. The same can be perceived in platform ruptures in socials, video streaming and other sectors, where we find fundamental (but productive) disagreements around competing normative thresholds.
