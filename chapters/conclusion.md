# Conclusion

One recurring observation throughout this thesis is that controversies over moderation rarely remain controversies about moderation alone. Whether the object of dispute is extremist speech, immigration, election integrity, COVID-19 treatments, political violence or misinformation, a conflict initially framed as a disagreement over content frequently becomes a disagreement over the means and norms of moderation itself [@chongCompetingNormsFree2018b]. This reflects a larger rupture in the composition of public spheres, where a multiplication of actors defines, exercises and eventually “platformizes” different notions and levels of tolerance for what constitutes acceptable language. This brings us back to the central question posed at the beginning of the thesis: how does moderation govern public speech when the norms it materializes are contested?

The literature reviewed in each part offers several familiar ways of answering this question. In [Part I](#/part-1), moderation is largely approached as a problem of platform governance: who governs platforms, through which instruments and with what legitimacy [@gorwaWhatPlatformGovernance2019b; @nielsenPowerPlatforms2022]. In [Part II](#/part-2), it is studied primarily through the drafting and application of policies, particularly the categories of harmful content they define and the enforcement systems used to apply them [@katzenbachPlatformGovernanceArchive2023]. The literature addressed in [Part III](#/part-3) more often studies online disorder through the platform’s capacity to identify harms, translate them into policy categories and secure compliance with those categories [@jiangCharacterizingCommunityGuidelines2020; @passmannHealthyCommunitiesToxic2023b; @mhortaribeiroAutomatedContentModeration2023]. [Part IV](#/part-4) engages accounts of alt-tech as the construction of alternative subcultures, rival models of the Internet and parallel technical infrastructures [@oharaFourInternetsData2021; @donovanNavigatingTechStack2019]. Finally, [Part V](#/part-5) takes up scholarship on the recent “free speech” turn at X and Meta through its consequences: a persistent increase in hate speech at X and a shift from consensus-building toward political deal-making at Meta [@hickeyMusksLeadershipSubstantial2025; @wattArtPlatformDeal2025].

Historically associated with restraint, temperance, mediation, and the management of debate [@lrabinowitzFindingModerationPlatos2023; @cthuderozModerationTout], I propose that moderation is less concerned with *preventing* communication than with *maintaining* communicative order under conditions in which exclusion alone becomes insufficient. While censorship addresses the problem of controlling publication, moderation addresses the problem of governing communication once publication becomes widely accessible. This distinction becomes particularly relevant in nominally open and pluralistic environments where speech cannot simply be prevented from circulating and where a central normative question becomes: how does one censor without censoring [@bourdieuLangagePouvoirSymbolique2001; @jansensuecurryCensorshipKnotThat1988]? 

Initially, *content* moderation emerges not only as a means of limiting communication but also as a means of maintaining the conditions under which communication is possible in plurivocal environments [@edwardsModeratorEmergingDemocratic2002; @wrightDemocracyDeliberationDesign2007b]. The task of the moderator, as highlighted later by @grimmelmannVirtuesModeration2015, depends on ensuring what I call “socio-informational coherence”: the epistemic conditions that enable participants to understand and exchange with one another. A fundamental feature of maintaining the legitimacy of the moderator as such is *metamoderation*, i.e., the moderation of debates around the norms on which moderation rules are based. As platforms centralized many of these affordances into the hands of their trust and safety teams, the legitimacy of moderation as such suffered from a series of crises about how and why platforms moderate the way they do.

On the other hand, moderation is also a *restraint* mechanism. Owing to its philosophical origins, it suggests a measure to restrict or reduce “excesses” — however defined in time and place — in a given threshold of tolerance. Moderation is known as such through marginalization, obfuscation, suspension or embargoing techniques, all of which seek to minimize the removal of information as a first resort. These techniques are “censorial” to the extent that they also engage in correcting, editing, modifying and eventually removing information based on fundamentally normative criteria. But in its history online, we see that they are often combined with the practice of moderation as a debate management mechanism far from adjudication alone. 

The history of moderation as a speech management technique also invites moderation scholars to engage with methods once honed for the study of other such techniques in different media. Scholars of censorship [@darntonCensorshipComparativePerspective1994; @mooreCensorship2016a; @postCensorshipSilencingPractices1998], publishing history and literary editing [@holquistIntroductionCorruptOriginals1994] have frequently reconstructed speech management interventions through their *traces*. Building on this tradition, Rogers and I proposed a framework for reconstructing what we termed the “scene of disappearance” of moderated content distributed across policies, infrastructures, enforcement mechanisms, user adaptations, visibility systems, and patterns of circulation. A broader range of empirical material becomes relevant: policy archives, visibility changes, recommendation systems, user responses, evasive vocabularies, migration patterns, alternative platforms, and emerging speech cultures all become constitutive pieces of environments constituted and moved by moderation effects.

The constitutive aspect of moderation is developed in [Part II. Speech affordances](#/part-2) through the concept of *affordances*. While affordance theory has often focused on what technologies may or may not allow users to do [@bucherAffordancesSocialMedia2018], the chapters in this section approached affordances as *constitutive* of platforms themselves [@gillespietarletonCustodiansInternet2018]. Empirically and conceptually, the question then shifts to what forms of communication become sayable under different moderation regimes and what speech cultures develop from (or in spite of) it. One consequence of this approach, for example, is that moderation is no longer understood solely in relation to *individual* platforms, but networks of platforms that become interdependent within a market of more or less allowed speech. 

This observation emerges in [Chapter 3](#/chapter-3) and [Chapter 4](#/chapter-4), which examined how the moderation of ambiguous online content formed various interdependencies between platforms with more or less permissible moderation policies. In this context, moderate speech undergoes processes of displacement and transformation: messages acquire euphemistic forms, are translated into humor, irony or ambiguity. They change form as they migrate between platforms possessing different moderation regimes and thresholds of acceptability. As a measure for the containment of undesired culture, this phenomenon of “displacement” shows how moderation tends to scaffold the moderated object across various “subterfuges” [@holquistIntroductionCorruptOriginals1994] that afford its different expressions.  

The same observation emerged from the analysis of large language models in [Chapter 5](#/chapter-5). Although, thus far, AI alignment tends to be treated as a separate field of inquiry, many of its underlying questions do indeed concern speech governance. Here, the question of speech affordances asks that we study how speech moderation is operationalized through the relatively different architecture that is alignment — through its training, data curation, red teaming and benchmarking processes; through its internal normative systems, to think of moderation APIs and other ways of capturing “risk” as a prospective form of objectionability [@gabrielArtificialIntelligenceValues2020]. Like social media platforms, models may be delimited by a particular threshold of tolerance that “accommodates some expressive modes and not others” [@fraserRethinkingPublicSphere1990]. 

The crises that such tensions sometimes give way to — those examined throughout [Part III](#/part-3) — reveal that the challenge facing platforms is not just one of enforcement but the management of disagreement over the speech norms that constitute moderation standards. Categories such as hate speech, misinformation, incitement, harassment, or discrimination, as well as the ways they are operationalized in policies, enforcement and alignment procedures, tend to presume a degree of stability in how those categories are understood. But the empirical material examined here reflects how the meanings attached to these categories remain at all times the subject of profound contestation.

[Chapter 6](#/chapter-6), for example, argues that such crises emerge when the assumptions underpinning moderation cease to command sufficient consensus among the populations subjected to them. Of course, what constitutes acceptable content evolves over time and remains subject to continuous renegotiation in user debates. In the absence of consensus-building mechanisms — where the platform may implement rules without sufficient public deliberation — moderation then becomes implicated in disputes over language itself. Such disagreements cannot be reduced to factual disputes because they concern competing understandings of the normative frameworks through which public communication ought to be organized in the first place [@gallieIXEssentiallyContestedConcepts1956]. Viewed from this perspective, it becomes clear that moderation participates in broader societal processes through which communities negotiate their terms of debate and decide what expression become unacceptable historically. 

[Chapter 7](#/chapter-7) demonstrated how these tensions emerge across different media environments. The refugee crisis provided a particularly useful case because it brought into confrontation several competing understandings of public speech norms — some articulated by Facebook and Twitter users, and others by television broadcasters. Discourses on the crisis diverged around the norms by which to report, describe and interpret the crisis as an event. Descriptions of immigration processes became in part disagreements over what constitutes xenophobia, racism, discrimination, tolerance, and inclusion. One consequence was that users shifted their media consumption toward alternative outlets whose editorial philosophy set a different threshold of tolerance that would cover specifically what is not considered “reportable” by competitors. This observation connects to the broader argument developed throughout [Part III](#/part-3) and [IV](#/part-4): that fragmentation emerges not so much because actors disagree about matters of substance around facts and ideas, but over the normative frameworks that bind such items to their admissible expression.

There are also, as the literature in platform governance is keen to highlight, crises of governance. Unlike the refugee debates, where disagreement concerned the interpretation of journalistic norms, [Chapter 8](#/chapter-8) shows how the moderation of content relating to the COVID-19 pandemic became subject to many crises of authority. One such crisis was epistemic: in the disorder of an “infodemic” [@raduFightingInfodemicLegal2020b] of competing statements and experiences of the pandemic, platforms relied on local and public health authorities to be the “authoritative sources” with which to determine the validity and visibility of COVID information. Yet, these authorities frequently disagreed about what treatments, transmission mechanisms, preventative measures, and public-health interventions to recommend, both among and within themselves. This created a vacuum of authority that placed platforms (here specifically, Twitter) in the unusual position to adjudicate between competing claims to epistemic validity, inadvertently transforming them into “accidental authorities” of their own. 

The implications of these institutional conflicts unfold in the case study of [Chapter 9](#/chapter-9). There, the concept of normative dislocation was introduced to describe situations in which moderation systems developed within one political and historical context are applied to another. Around the Brazilian elections of 2022, moderation tended to be framed through the language of misinformation, electoral integrity, and democratic resilience. Yet these categories are inevitably molded by a historical environment marked by different normative criteria. Militaristic discourse, for example, was in the eyes of the Brazilian judiciary a clear type of objectionable content due to the historical memory of a military dictatorship, as well as the redemocratization and transitional (or retributive) justice processes that followed. This type of discourse was *not* moderated; what was removed, flagged or demoted were violations of a policy developed specifically for previous US elections. The political and institutional crisis that ensued on the ground showed how platform moderation became inseparable from questions of historical memory. Decisions concerning what information should circulate became decisions concerning which interpretations of the past remain publicly legitimate, and how to mediate them as they erupted into profound political polarization. 

The consequences of the crises described above become visible in [Part IV](#/part-4). The notion of “platform ruptures” describes a reorganization of communication spaces due to irresolvable or “deep” [@fogelinLogicDeepDisagreements1985] disagreements over moderation norms. Such disagreements, as elaborated in [Chapter 10](#/chapter-10), give way to alternative platforms that reconstitute themselves by the speech affordances their predecessors would not grant. While these environments are often described as part of a process of online fragmentation, one could see them as an effort to platformize their content and affordances, in the same logic as mainstream competitors. Between them persist speech markets that, though characterized by different moderation regimes and thresholds of tolerance, complement each other through their respective limitations. This is apparent in [Chapter 12](#/chapter-12), where one sees deplatformed YouTube users continue to use the platform as a source of primary conspiratorial material, while displacing conspiratorial deliberation to BitChute. It is also appearent in [Chapter 11](#/chapter-11), where Metapedia, Infogalactic and Rightpedia sought to replatform alternative epistemic principles under the exact same platformed logic as Wikipedia’s. 

If many of the crises and ruptures described throughout the preceding chapters emerge because platforms have become increasingly involved in adjudicating disputes over public norms, the question therefore becomes how moderation can operate under conditions where consensus can no longer be assumed. [Chapter 13](#/chapter-13) and [14](#/chapter-14) suggest that part of the answer lies in a gradual transformation of the logic of moderation itself. Throughout much of the past decade, moderation has frequently been organized around platforms identifying objectionable content, determining whether it violates platform rules, and intervening accordingly. While the sophistication of the technical systems involved has increased, one of the limitations of this model is an inevitable problem of normative demarcation [@vanraemdonckConceptualFrameworkMutual2022] — that is, where and why to delimit their threshold of tolerance. Where this threshold ends, a platform is confronted to its finitude, be it ideological partiality or limited speech affordances users may complement elsewhere. 

[Chapter 13](#/chapter-13) explored how Twitter responded to this challenge through what my co-authors and I described as “modulated moderation” that, rather than treating objectionability as a fixed property of content, attunes its interventions based on how objectionable a type of content may be in a time and place. Demotion occupies a particularly important place within this framework because it allows platforms to intervene relatively agnostically, i.e., relative to dynamic and contradictory public perceptions of objectionability. In turn, [Chapter 14](#/chapter-14) maps this shift as part of a broader transformation in platform moderation philosophies. Community Notes arguably emerges from a recognition that moderation disputes concern the consensus and legitimacy of those responsible for evaluating it. Community Notes attempts to identify agreement across groups that would ordinarily disagree with one another, attempting to moderate based on whatever consensus is generated among users.

In this respect, Community Notes can be understood as one example of a broader family of moderation techniques that seek to distribute the work of judgment across publics, rather than concentrating it within platforms. Such systems do remain experimental and produce exclusions of their own. Still, they begin to return to an earlier ethos of moderation as the *facilitation of public debate* supported by consensus-building metamoderation affordances. One of the arguments developed in [Chapter 1](#/chapter-1) was that a purpose of moderation is to ensure that disagreement remained possible without collapsing the conditions of exchange. While contemporary platforms differ enormously from the communicative environments in which these earlier understandings emerged, the underlying problem returns: how can communication be pursued among actors who disagree not only about substantive questions, but also about the very terms through which those questions should be discussed?

The developments examined in Chapters 13 and 14 do not provide a definitive answer to this question, but do indicate that moderation may be gradually returning to what [Chapter 1](#/chapter-1) called a “debate management system”. This shift is visible in the language of “prosocial” moderation systems [@tangEmbraceShiftProsocial2024], the emergence of bridging systems [@ovadyaBridgingSystemsOpen2023], distributed evaluation mechanisms [@lampeSlashdotBurnDistributed2004; @allenBirdsFeatherDont2022a], and other experiments designed to facilitate contact between actors occupying different normative environments. They invite a reconsideration of moderation as a public-interest infrastructure to maintain communicative relations between groups that may otherwise possess few opportunities for intelligible dialogue. Throughout the empirical material examined here, for example, ruptured publics rarely ceased orienting themselves toward one another. The persistence of these relations suggests that platform ruptures do not necessarily eliminate them, but if anything, reveal how difficult it is to escape them. It raises the possibility that the many fragmentations perceived in modern public spheres may actually be a reorganization of contact between political cultures that previously interacted under very different conditions, if any at all.

Indeed, one alternative reading of the period examined throughout this thesis is that it represents not just a crisis of public communication but an *expansion* of it. Positions that had previously remained marginal, geographically dispersed, institutionally excluded, or culturally stigmatized acquire new communicative environments. The confrontations that ensue are indeed uncomfortable, but they create unique opportunities for exchanges previously overlooked or impossible. In turn, such exchanges allow for a continuous transformation of moderation as the practice of making dialogue possible. There is no ease to it, nor is it fair to claim that all forms of dialogue are desirable; the history of political thought contains many reasons to be skeptical of naïve assumptions concerning rational consensus [@mouffeAgonisticsThinkingWorld2013; @popperOpenSocietyIts1971]. Yet they do not convince us that dialogue is in itself impossible. They only show that, under these novel circumstances, moderation can and ought to be innovated to facilitate exchanges we thought improbable, impossible or even unacceptable.  

Part of this transformation is already visible through research on so-called “prosocial media” [@weylCommunityDesign2026a; @dorrResearchAgendaEncouraging2025b; @schirchBlueprintProsocialTech2025], bridging systems, deliberative technologies and collective sensemaking — topics still relatively separate from platform governance, at least academically. But new initiatives do explore how principles drawn from dialogue studies and conflict resolution might inform technical design, and organizations working on social cohesion begin to engage directly with questions of platform architecture. This involves scholars of democratic theory, peace and conflict mediation studies, communication and computer science who accept common responsibilities to define and propose the conditions for online public dialogue. Of course, whether such efforts will succeed, or what “success” means here, remains uncertain. But the question they pose is likely to remain one of the defining questions of contemporary communication in massive plural environments: not how disagreement may be “resolved”, but under what conditions and norms they may be sustainable. Moderation, on this account, does not adjudicate the quarrel but keeps it from collapsing the space in which it occurs.
