# Abstract

In Internet studies, content moderation is typically framed as a question of platform regulation and governance. Yet, the etymology and older media uses of the term give reason to treat moderation more broadly, as one among several speech governance techniques — alongside censorship, gatekeeping, editorial convention and, latterly, AI alignment — through which public discourse is managed in relation to historically derived norms. I argue that moderation can be understood as a speech management mechanism designed for plurivocal environments and organized around two core functions. *Enframing and enforcement* establish the norms of debate while intervening as little as possible, using modular and dynamic techniques that label, obfuscate, demote or otherwise marginalize objectionable speech before removal becomes necessary. *Facilitation* maintains socio-informational coherence — the possibility for participants in an informational environment to understand one another. [Part I. What is moderation?](#/part-1) develops this definition and the trace-based methodology required to study an ephemeral practice. [Part II. Speech affordances](#/part-2) shows how these functions become constitutive of platforms and other information and communication technologies, materializing their boundaries of permissible speech as *speech affordances*. The facilitative function has been reformed by platforms primarily concerned with moderation at scale and organized around simpler epistemic categories such as relevance, popularity and freshness. This transformation has produced fundamental clashes examined in [Part III. Moderation crises](#/part-3): conflicts over thresholds of tolerance, definitions of acceptable speech and their normative premises, and the actors, institutions and contexts entitled to decide. [Part IV. Ruptures](#/part-4) follows what happens when those clashes can no longer be held within a common environment and reorganize speech, users and knowledge into counter-spheres. [Part V. Modulation and consensus](#/part-5) examines two platform responses: normatively agnostic developments of moderation’s modular and dynamic repertoire, and consensus-building systems that make agreement among users part of a moderation decision. Methodologically, the thesis develops trace research, dynamic archiving, the moderation stack, longitudinal and cross-platform reconstruction, cross-media comparison, multilingual distant reading and LLM perspectivism to study moderation through the secondary metadata and changing states it leaves behind.
