# Acknowledgments

People often describe the experience of writing a thesis as a road: long and winding, as the song goes, or long and so tortuous it teaches the young student questions of pace and patience. 

There has been a road to writing this one, too, but it’s my encounter with friends, colleagues or both that made the journey. 

It started on what must have been a winter evening. I walked into Bernhard Rieder’s office and he made the subtle effort to light just one lamp against one of those tall, damp walls of BG1, at the University of Amsterdam.

He said: “You’re going to want to do a PhD. You will go on in life wanting to write something.” 

Today he apologizes for encouraging me, but I take no offense. I didn’t start right away, but the desire arose when so much of the world changed that there was a sense of freedom in trying to understand it. And there were so many friends with whom to try.

I met Rhubi and Maria when a five-minute conversation about God knows what class assignment became a debate about the need for diplomacy between India and Pakistan, and how diplomacy should be done online because, by then, social media was already inflaming our feeds. Though we only spent a year together hanging out, it’s been the beginning of a lifetime since those conversations start again.

I met Sal, Emilija and Marc, with whom we created a little lab that kick-started our academic careers. In the fashion of digital methods, we built things we had no idea made sense to journalists, Google and government people. What a crazy idea it was from Marc to invite us, students, into this endeavor; and what a crazy amount of gratitude I have for him to have given us the benefit of the doubt. 

I met Julia, the CLARIAH and CREATE team — Leon, Liliana, Jasmijn, Thomas, MRJ and so many with whom I could mess around in academia even when walking over to the Dean’s office for a presentation on the future of the humanities at UvA. Under Julia’s watchful gaze everything seemed a little kinder and easier, even when I in fact messed up. 

I met Jonathan and Wendy, the Public Data Lab and Digital Democracy team, who were the first to welcome me into a PhD program (this thing has been going on since 2021!). Someone who, like Wendy, had everything except time and gave it to those who, like me, don’t know what to make of it. A conversation with her was a gift, the same one she gave me even when I decided to return to the Netherlands. 

Jonathan would say: “You need to find a sense of time”, or “space”, or both. It was in the middle of COVID-19 and Liliana was not doing well, but both gave to me what they give to all: so much kindness. 

I met Bharath at a conference in London, on the perfectly cut green grass of Richmond University. He looked perplexed: he was heading to Groningen after a stay in Oxford, so I thought I’d see him there. A year or two later I emailed him 24h before a PhD application asking him to supervise me: “Dear Ganesh”, I said, and in spite of studying all the shapes and sounds of racism the man took no offense. I’ve tested his patience many times over, and many times over he responded with the same generosity. That generosity is what carried this PhD to its finish line. We’ve come to the conclusion that we do, in fact, agree more than we think we don’t; and when we don’t, I learn I have something to learn from him. 

I met Marcel when Thomas asked me if I’d be interested in teaching in Groningen. “Will you ride the train to Groningen?” is a question I think Marcel has secretly asked me many times since, and though I haven’t always kept my ideal pace, he always waited at the other end. Without expectations, I always found him when I needed; and even when I didn’t, I found a friend.

I met Richard as a student, and as the student I was I asked him: “But do you actually participate in data sprints?” — and that became a ruse I’m thankful he forgot (and here I am reminding him). We used to call him the Mick Jagger of the teachers because he was so cool, and though the comment was mostly about aesthetics, it was valid because Richard is also kind of an artist. There are moments where the creativity is his and for me to admire, and others where we share intuitions and a sense of understanding. That sense is a rare find in this road, and one I keep when I think alone.

When I headed to Boston, I met Tarleton. On Mondays he sat in his office for the first few hours of the day, and then he headed off to a café. I thought I had decoded the magic of his writings: it must have been the coffee, or the routine. But when I sat in his office I think I understood: it was an extraordinary willingness to understand others and let himself be understood. Suddenly, my semi-articulated thoughts were welcomed into a conversation of ideas, and ideas from ideas. How lucky I’ve been to meet him. 

In Copenhagen, Rasmus and Diyi waited with patience for me to finish this thesis even when I told them I wasn’t working on it any longer. Stine, with her fairy charm, offered a help I didn’t even consider possible given her responsibilities. Friends were there, too, especially when I thought I was alone: Ece, Eva, CJ, Rebecca, Emil, Victoria, Jasper, Kiran, Filippa, Nadja. 

Online, at work, through friends and conferences I’ve met Marcelo, João, Anthony, Stijn, Daniël and Daniel, Thomas, Anne, Clare, Peter, Natalia, Erik, Esther, Marloes, Fernando, Daniël, Dale, Stefania, Lonneke, Janna, Giulia, Thais, Chico, Cass, Guillén, Max, Tom, Sarah, Lisa, Ravi, Luke, Julie, Jonathan Stray, the Council for Tech and Social Cohesion. At Microsoft I’ve met Mary, the Nancies, Zaidat, Emily, Chuncheng, Taj, Parker, Ryland, Tobias and the gang. At the UN I met Daanish, Naoko, Minji, Martin, Oleh, Zurab, Davit, Alex and the UN DPPA Georgia team. 

There were friends from older roads along the way: Louis, Kiki, Lana, Kevin, Chiara, Alex, Anne, Liam, Santi, Alaíde, mãe, Astrid, Cristel, Emeline. 

Along one I thought I wouldn't continue I met Maud, and it continues thanks to her.

And I met Ivan. He was sitting at his desk eating his usual Dutch sandwich for a maximum of 15 minutes when I asked him, an archaeologist, if he had ever heard of media archaeology. That conversation turned into a friendship, and that friendship into adventures afar in the world.

Ivan shows up many times in this thesis because, if I didn’t have to write it, it would be his. The whole is his doing: the method, the code, the time late in the night answering my Slack messages a day prior to a conference presentation. The presence to listen and respond together. 

Ivan left, but this thesis is the road we walked together. It is dedicated to him.
