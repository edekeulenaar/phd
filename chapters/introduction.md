# Introduction

One common denominator of Internet studies is an interest in how social media platforms reconfigure conditions for public debate. Depending, of course, on the geopolitical vantage point a study inadvertently adopts, many platforms were once celebrated for “liberalizing” political expression from the editorial gatekeeping of legacy media. The Arab Spring was for a time an emblematic case study of how platforms would turn individual expression into a form of democratic participation [@howardDemocracysFourthWave2013]. Decades later, these same mechanisms would liberate “problematic” actors and ideas until then relatively marginalized in public debate [@mungerRightWingYouTubeSupply2022], to think of political forces that straddled the norms of public expression in places long assumed to be mature democracies [@eatwellNationalPopulismRevolt2018].

One diagnosis of this transformation has tended to frame platforms as producing *excessive information*. On this account, the relaxation of editorial gatekeeping allows bad actors to re-emerge in public and at times corrupt public opinion through manipulation and coordinated influence, all while pushing the boundaries of platform policies toward a sense of epistemic and moral relativism. The implicit critique is that the often arbitrary and forbidding gatekeeping forces of the past at least ensured some basic standards of informational integrity and cohesion [@tuckerSocialMediaPolitical2018a]. Their sudden dismantlement set the scene for a variety of “information disorders” [@socialscienceresearchcouncilHateSpeechInformation2020] where incommensurably diverse perspectives clash and produce a general atmosphere of toxicity, particularly as a form of misinformation and hostile language, however defined.

From a regulatory perspective, a good many responses have consisted in honing a more decisive answer to what counts as acceptable speech in the first place. Much has been said of EU and (as of late) Brazilian platform regulation as quests to recover a basic sense of normalcy to public expression, particularly by reinforcing editorial norms against discrimination, sedition and attempts to overthrow key institutions [@europeanunionRegulationEU20222022]. But with the level of public debate around such legislation, one can place these agreements within an otherwise constantly evolving and contentious timeline of debates on what is objectionable, how and to whom. One of the few constants to emerge from this timeline is that speech norms remain “in constant negotiation” [@fraserRethinkingPublicSphere1990] over which aspects of history may or may not be allowed to recur through language and behavior [see @chongCompetingNormsFree2018b]. 

Among the contenders in this debate are platforms themselves. In response to the “massive pluralism” [@weylCommunityDesign2026a] of their user base, social media have somewhat inadvertently centralized the means to manage and define acceptable public speech [@sabloskyDangerousOrganizationsFacebooks2021], at least for a time. Distinct from federalized or community-based moderation systems [@rozenshteinModeratingFediverseContent2023], the majority of social media platforms do often hold the last word around the moderation of their own services. This has attracted accusations of bypassing a necessary negotiation with publics and elected speech governing actors; a step some have described as platform “totalitarianism” [@diamondRoadDigitalUnfreedom2019] and “authoritarianism” [@magalhaesGreatSysopElon2026; @rauchbergPlatformAuthoritarianismCreator2026]. More fundamental interrogations bear on the nature of platform power relative to that of whatever is deemed “traditional” in the wider history of public governance, to name states [@klonickNewGovernorsPeople2017; @badouardRegulationContenusInternet2020] and civil society [@balkinFreeSpeechTriangle2018].

But while platforms hold many of the means of speech governance, they have also redistributed governance affordances among the actors they host [@rbadouardPlateformesNouveauxCenseurs]. So emerges a strange paradox. Platforms may well have expanded the participative affordances of public expression, including the possibilities for communities to manage their own exchanges [@edwardsModeratorEmergingDemocratic2002]. But they have also become sites *and* actors of conflicts about the very terms of public debate. 

For one, censorial powers have been redistributed and to a certain extent democratized as a _private_ right [@cookTwoTypesSelfCensorship2013; @messinaPrivateCensorship2023] granted to a variety of actors [@gyetvanCensorshipFreedomExpression2025; @amadieuFormesContemporainesCensure2020], who may themselves grant the right affordances for other groups to exercise censorial powers of their own. These are censorial affordances in a descriptive sense, as means to act upon the access, visibility and circulation of other speech, even when their purpose is simply to sustain a community rather than repress opposing viewpoints. At an infrastructural level, we may find states, cloud service providers or app stores removing access to entire platforms. At the level of the service, trust and safety teams draft and enforce the terms under which everyone else speaks. Within and by way of platforms, we will find “more or less ephemeral associations […], groups or subgroups” [@amadieuFormesContemporainesCensure2020, p. 91] exercising pressure or “cancel” campaigns against another group, issue or cultural asset; and at the finest grain, individual users muting, blocking, reporting and deleting  [@meisnerWeaponizationPlatformGovernance2023; @jiangTradeoffcenteredFrameworkContent2023]. 

There are also fundamental disagreements on what constitutes objectionable information, and on the basis of which personal and collective memory [@chongCompetingNormsFree2018b]. The result is competing speech norms, each practicing a more or less exclusive understanding of what counts as objectionable under different thresholds of tolerance. Platforms themselves tend to align more or less with one or another premise, operationalised into a variety of enforcement techniques often in direct confrontation with states, civil society organisations, advertisers and other actors seeking to orient policy according to norms of their own [@balkinFreeSpeechTriangle2018]. 

**How, then, does moderation work when the very norms it is subject to become contested?** 

That this question emerges so frequently in public debate may have come as something of a surprise to platforms as products of a profound sense of liberalism — projects to aggregate the greatest possible number of users into a single speech market. Within the history of the Web, they restate an older proposition that history, understood as a sequence of fundamental ideological confrontations, resolves itself through consensual deliberation as the main engine of liberal-democratic politics [@fukuyamaEndHistoryLast2006] — provided the tolerance extended to freedom of thought is generous enough. What was less anticipated is their limitation precisely at competing and oftentimes irreducibly different thresholds of tolerance, whether with other governing actors, with their own users, or with their competitors. The disagreement is not so much about what may be said but about how much may be borne.

Though the capacity to moderate speech based on one's private criteria constitutes a freedom in its own right, it also demonstrates the relative absence of consensus-building mechanisms for publics to coalesce around the terms of their own debate. It is a problem less about what platforms ought and ought not to tolerate — and what states ought and ought not to restrict through them — than about how citizens can come to some agreement about the very terms of public debate in platformized public spheres. 

Throughout this thesis, I call *speech norms* these more or less explicit agreements. Unless they are decided and enforced by authority [@allanForbiddenWordsTaboo2006], they can result from explicit or implicit social, cultural and political processes that produce a common (but certainly variable) understanding of why we agree to speak to one another the way we do. Readers of the philosophy of communication may recognize the notion of “understanding” here as resembling Habermas’ notion of the “lifeworld” of dialogue [@habermasContributionsDiscourseTheory2020, p. 14], i.e., the background information used to make sense of the norms of linguistic interaction. One may think of memorial and anti-discrimination laws as efforts to canonize historical lessons on “words that hurt”: speech is seen as conjuring, and thus re-legitimizing circumstances from the past [@butlerRuledOutVocabularies1998] that the state [@levinsonSilencingPublicMonuments] or communities of people [@matsudaWordsThatWound2018] “never again” wish to be repeated. All around may lay monuments, stories, policies and political discourses to explain why as a form of remembrance, reconciliation or retributive justice [@ricoeurMemoireHistoireOubli2006a]. Statues may be overthrown and flags changed, an idea or public figure canceled or redeemed, and in all cases new agreements are produced on what words mean and why and how they ought to be used in a particular way. 

These agreements also *do* a great deal. They can be prescriptive, in the sense that they move efforts to reform culture, social relations and political processes, to think of decommunization, derussification [@greenLanguageLullabiesRussification1997], denazification [@vincentPunirReeduquerProcess2008], or (re)democratization processes foreclosing military regimes [@vizquadratHistoriaMemoriaPolitica2016]. A key pretext is to redirect and otherwise transform speech in ways conducive to historical changes [@mcintoshLanguageTrumpEra2020], based on the aspirations or prescriptions of whatever collective. To them, language becomes a vector of change by virtue of its power to designate what can and cannot be said on what is and can be. It also becomes a means to manage what *is* against what *could* be, as is the case with German anti-extremism laws acting as democratic “militancy”, or “self-defense” [@loewensteinMilitantDemocracyFundamental1937]. Crystallized into legislation, institutional frameworks and political culture, these discursive techniques constitute memorial levers by which individuals presently act upon their collective past. 

Speech norms also hold a constitutive force: they materialize into “discursive architectures” [@mecheverriaArquitecturaDiscursivaPlataformas, p. 2] that “accommodate some expressive modes and not others” [@fraserRethinkingPublicSphere1990]. These may be in “legacy” media [@martinCensuresDansMonde2016], in platforms of all sizes [@gillespietarletonCustodiansInternet2018] and kinds [@gorwaModeratingModelMarketplaces2024b], and more fundamentally in language itself as a primary vector of social norms [@allanForbiddenWordsTaboo2006; @bourdieuLangagePouvoirSymbolique2001]. In turn, these norms give way to various affordances or possibilities for expression [@bucherAffordancesSocialMedia2018]. Some are proper to censorious regimes [@postCensorshipSilencingPractices1998], others to the facilitation of public debate [@habermasNewStructuralTransformation2023], to maintaining editorial norms that “gatekeep” [@shoemakerGatekeepingTheory2009a], fact-check and otherwise edit information; and as of late, that train AIs to speak one or another way [@gabrielArtificialIntelligenceValues2020]. For every limitation there is a corresponding affordance: nooks and crannies through which speech escapes to wherever it can best be expressed [@dezeeuwTracingNormieficationCrossplatform2020], camouflaging itself, adapting, or taking the shape opposite to the restraint it evades.

For spheres to remain "public" (in the political sense of the word), they require the means to build and maintain agreement around their very norms of expression. Different actors and mechanisms have, over time, facilitated the production of such agreements about the meaning and use of words. They include political and legal institutions, editorial conventions, journalists, churches, schools, associations and the many informal authorities through which a public learns why certain expressions ought to be accepted, restrained or rejected. Such processes are of course never entirely achievable, because at issue are norms that are *essentially* contested [@delaatCoercionEmpowermentModeration2012], and, as above, "in constant negotiation" [@fraserRethinkingPublicSphere1990]. Negotiations may fall apart into "deep disagreements" [@fogelinLogicDeepDisagreements1985] that cannot be resolved with authority, be it epistemic or normative. Consensus, then, need not be confused with unanimity, but with the *ongoing* process by which speakers deliberate and arrive at *relative* understanding of the meanings, procedures and authorities of the norms by which they engage in public debate.

The need for consensus-building around speech norms is not foreign to moderation. By name, moderation is indeed the art of managing public debate [@oxfordenglishdictionaryModerationMeaningsEtymology2025]. As a practice, it emerged as speech management technique designed to maintain intelligibility and cohesion in essential pluralistic informational environments [@grimmelmannVirtuesModeration2015], where the goal is to maintain a sense of epistemic and social coherence. To do so, moderation must “censor without censoring” [@jansensuecurryCensorshipKnotThat1988]: it ought to maintain the production and exchange of information, but within boundaries that render it socially or epistemically coherent. This means *enframing* an information environment within finite normative and epistemic boundaries (of intelligibility, coherence, civility, or other), but with essentially dynamic, modular and almost "invisible" [@rouveyrolRoleComplexeModerateur2020] mechanisms that try to interrupt information flows. This also means *facilitating* such flows. The moderator prompts a conversation, contextualizes, mediates, redirects, corrects, balances, summarises conversation. Early writings on *content* moderation described precisely this work of maintaining exchange and intelligibility in online fora [@edwardsModeratorEmergingDemocratic2002; @wrightDemocracyDeliberationDesign2007b].

This facilitative task appears to have gained less favor than that of moderation as *enforcement*, as platforms have been compelled to develop moderation “at scale” [@gillespieContentModerationAI2020] in response to major scandals, regulatory demands and a growing repertoire of legally objectionable content [@hillDefinitelyNotBusiness2024]. The more dialogical work of moderation as *debate management* was then partly displaced into algorithmic systems with simpler epistemic categories, such as relevance, popularity or freshness. This, in part, has led to a series of *moderation crises* — of the practice, for one, but also of the norms it imposes in the absence of consensus-building affordances. 

I pursue five related subquestions, each corresponding to one part of the thesis. 

In [[#Part I. What is moderation?|Part I. What is moderation?]], I ask: what kind of speech governance technique is moderation in the context of other techniques, such as censorship, gatekeeping, or debate management? How does the literature theorize speech moderation beyond new media studies, and what light do such definitions bring about how else speech moderation has been practiced in history, or otherwise today? 

Given its theoretical background, how does one set out to study moderation practices? How does one capture moderation when, like censorial practices, it tends to only leave *traces* behind rather than explicit interventions? 

In [[#Part II. Speech affordances|Part II. Speech affordances]], the guiding question is: how does moderation come to constitute platforms and platform speech cultures? How does it shape the circulation of more or less acceptable information across platforms and platform spaces (for example, commenting spheres)? 

In [[#Part III. Moderation crises|Part III. Moderation crises]], I ask: how do clashes emerge among users, platforms, media and institutions around different thresholds of tolerance, definitions of acceptable speech and the reasons for those definitions? How do differences of authority and historical context turn such clashes into crises of moderation?

In [[#Part IV. Ruptures|Part IV. Ruptures]], I then ask: when do moderation crises become ruptures, reorganizing speech, users and knowledge into online counter-spheres? What relations do those counter-spheres continue to maintain with the platforms from which they emerged?

Finally, in [[#Part V. Modulation and consensus|Part V. Modulation and consensus]], I examine two responses platforms have developed to moderation crises: a normatively agnostic development of its modular and dynamic enforcement techniques, adjusting intervention to changing thresholds without settling them, and consensus-building systems such as Community Notes that make agreement among users part of a moderation decision.

Each of these parts and their chapters are detailed below. 

#### Part I. What is moderation?

[[#Part I. What is moderation?|Part I. What is moderation?]] establishes the conceptual and methodological ground for the thesis. 

[[#Chapter 1. Censorship and moderation|Chapter 1. Censorship and moderation]] begins from a confusion its object has come to embody — that “moderation” and “censorship” are now used almost interchangeably by platforms and their critics alike — and asks what kind of practice moderation is once that normative opposition is set aside. Reading content moderation studies alongside the literature on adjacent speech governance techniques, especially censorship, it proceeds through a distant reading [@morettiDistantReading2013] of a large corpus of scholarship on speech governance, from which I build a taxonomy of how moderation and censorship are defined, by whom they are practiced, why, and through which techniques. 

[[#Chapter 2. After deplatforming|Chapter 2. After deplatforming]] then asks how moderation can be studied empirically, developing a framework for reconstructing the “scene of disappearance” of moderated content, later extending the concern to the moderation behavior of LLMs in [[#Chapter 5. LLMs and the generation of moderate speech|Chapter 5. LLMs and the generation of moderate speech]]. Having mentioned various works from censorship studies in the previous chapter, it draws from the methods of censorship scholars. I focus on four components of moderation: its documentation in the form of platform policies; how such documents can be used to determine what is moderated and how; reverse-engineering various moderation practices, such as removal, flagging or demotion; and exploring how users react to such effects.

#### Part II. Speech affordances

[[#Part II. Speech affordances|Part II. Speech affordances]] examines how speech norms come to be materialized in the technical and (sub)cultural fabric of platforms. By “speech affordances”, I mean the policies, techniques, and user speech cultures that together signal what may and may not be said in a given space and thereby govern how content circulates within and across more- and less-moderated regions of the Web. 

[[#Chapter 3. The affordances of extreme speech|Chapter 3. The affordances of extreme speech]] theorizes this through the affordances of extreme speech, following the migration of a meme across 4chan, Twitter, and YouTube. Such affordances materialize what may and may not be said in a given platform (and how), and thereby forms the relation and information circulation across different spaces (for example, from “mainstream” to “alt-tech” spaces). 

In turn, [[#Chapter 4. The affordances of replacement narratives|Chapter 4. The affordances of replacement narratives]] develops a case study of “replacement” narratives, attending to the linguistic subterfuges — displacements, dissimulations, coded “transcodings” — through which a taboo is signaled without being stated. Both describe how speech norms materialize into speech affordances and form counter-spheres with competing affordances.

[[#Chapter 5. LLMs and the generation of moderate speech|Chapter 5. LLMs and the generation of moderate speech]] explores how speech norms are operationalized in LLM text generation. It is about how eight models (GPT 3.5 and 4, Llama2 and 3, Claude Sonnet and Haiku, and Mistral) respond to the top controversial questions posted on multilingual Q&A subreddits. I explore the discursive techniques each LLM uses — or what I call “moderate speech” — when responding to questions with varying degrees of controversy and risk, as defined by each model’s moderation API. 

#### Part III. Moderation crises

Having explained how speech norms become a formative component of platforms as public spheres, [[#Part III. Moderation crises|Part III. Moderation crises]] examines the consequences of two linked transformations: the reformation of moderation’s facilitative function by platforms moderating at scale, and the wider democratization of censorial affordances across contemporary public spheres. The chapters trace clashes between platforms and users, platforms and public institutions, editorial media and social media audiences, and global moderation standards and local historical processes. These are crises not merely because actors disagree about content, but because they bring different thresholds of tolerance, definitions of acceptable or truthful speech, reasons for those definitions, and claims to authority into the same communicative environment.

[[#Chapter 6. Moderation in crisis|Chapter 6. Moderation in crisis]] examines conflict between platforms and their publics through the case of YouTube comments between 2004 and 2022. The study is about how YouTube commenters define and dispute what is hateful, misinformation and otherwise unacceptable in the platform throughout 2004 and 2018, and how eventual disagreements shape the way users view the platform from an “open sphere” to a corporate entity. 

[[#Chapter 7. Of migrants and strangers|Chapter 7. Of migrants and strangers]] turns to the dissensus between platforms and the editorial norms of *legacy media*, following how Dutch broadcasters and their social media audiences diverged over the “refugee crisis” of 2013–2018. Here, my co-authors and I explore how social media publics and Dutch broadcasters begin to diverge around Dutch speech norms about discrimination in relation to the crisis (2015-2018). As users and broadcasters frame the refugee crisis in different normative lenses, they also began to produce mutually alternative facts about the crisis.

[[#Chapter 8. Twitter as an accidental authority|Chapter 8. Twitter as an accidental authority]] considers platforms and public health organizations, showing how Twitter became an inadvertent or “accident” authority while trying to surface authoritative information about COVID-19 transmission, treatment and prevention information through its fact-checking systems. As local health organizations and state authorities failed to maintain stable consensus over time and among themselves, Twitter steps in as an authority in its own right by moderating those public health authorities for allegedly spreading falsehoods. 

Finally, [[#Chapter 9. Normative dislocation|chapter 9]] addresses the dissensus between platforms and broader processes of transitional justice. It introduces the concept of *normative dislocation* to explain how US-rooted moderation standards failed to register local Brazilian prohibitions on militaristic speech during the 2022 elections by prioritizing electoral “misinformation” over open calls for a military coup. It shows how this “dislocation” allowed the spread of content once contained by legacy media, and threatened to maintain a fragile but key historical consensus against military political violence.

#### Part IV. Ruptures

[[#Part IV. Ruptures|Part IV. Ruptures]] considers what emerges from deep disagreements about content moderation norms. 

[[#Chapter 10. Platform ruptures and the formation of online counter spheres|Chapter 10. Platform ruptures and the formation of online counter spheres]] defines “alt-tech” platforms as counter-spheres that emerge from unresolved disagreements about speech norms. It charts a brief history of a few major normative ruptures that have seen the constitution of multiple alt-tech spheres across the Web, namely cloud hosts,  blogging sites, wikis, social media and messaging applications. It proposes the notion of “platform ruptures” to describe a complementary trend to “platformization” [@helmondPlatformizationWebMaking2015].

[[#Chapter 11. Epistemic ruptures in the encyclopedias of alternative facts|Chapter 11. Epistemic ruptures in the encyclopedias of alternative facts]] turns from technical and platform ruptures to epistemic ones, following how editors banned from Wikipedia fork its content into rival encyclopedias like Metapedia, Infogalactic and Rightpedia — Grokipedia did not exist just yet. Reading Wikipedia through Lyotard’s notion of postmodern conditions for knowledge production, it argues that the platform’s consensus-and-neutrality ideal meets a limit wherever disagreements prove irreconcilable. Alternative wikis arise at those fault lines not as a symptom of “post-truth” indifference to facts, but as attempts to give alternative epistemic viewpoints a platformed, and thus “universal”, format.

[[#Chapter 12. Deplatforming, demotion and folk theories of Big Tech persecution|Chapter 12. Deplatforming, demotion and folk theories of Big Tech persecution]] examines the obverse dynamic — that is, how deplatforming and demotion become entangled with the conspiratorial “folk theories” users develop about moderation itself during YouTube’s handling of COVID-19 “borderline content”. It is placed under this section because it illustrates how the users YouTube deplatforms do not stop using the platform, but simply change their uses in a way coherent with the narratives they were deplatformed for. Users sanctioned for COVID-19 conspiracy theories may post their original content on Bitchute but still use YouTube as a primary source of study — i.e., the place where conspirators dwell, including YouTube itself. 

### Part V. Modulation and consensus

[[#Part V. Modulation and consensus|Part V. Modulation and consensus]] looks at two families of platform response to the crises and ruptures addressed above: modular and dynamic enforcement techniques that remain relatively agnostic toward changing norms, and consensus-building mechanisms that attempt to reintroduce a facilitative element by making agreement among users part of a moderation decision.

[[#Chapter 13.  Modulating moderation|Chapter 13. Modulating moderation]] explores how objectionable content was defined by Twitter’s moderation practices from 2010 to shortly after Musk’s takeover. It argues that Twitter increasingly combined modular and dynamic techniques, particularly demotion, into what my co-authors and I call a “crisis-resistant” speech architecture. Its normative plasticity is a response to changing and contradictory thresholds of objectionability: the system can adjust visibility without finally resolving what makes a category unacceptable.

The final study, [[#Chapter 14. From Twitter to X|Chapter 14. From Twitter to X]], examines the second response. Community Notes makes cross-group agreement among raters a condition for attaching a note, relocating part of the moderation decision from platform adjudication to a consensus-building procedure. This resembles moderation’s facilitative function without necessarily restoring it: agreement about a note is not the same as deliberation about the norms and terms through which speech is governed.

### Methodologies

Throughout, the thesis operationalizes concepts from political philosophy, censorship studies and speech governance as digital methods [@rogersDigitalMethods2013]. The central empirical difficulty is that moderation is essentially ephemeral and most often survives as *secondary metadata*: a missing account, a changed status, a label, a ranking position, a policy revision or a link toward another platform. The primary intervention is rarely available for observation as such. The methods developed here therefore treat absence, modification and redistribution as evidence of platform behavior.

The first contribution is a renewed form of *trace research* that reverses the usual direction of platform analysis: rather than using platform traces to study users, it uses traces left on users and content to study the platform. *Dynamic archiving* captures repeated states of posts, search results and policies so that disappearance, demotion and relabelling become observable events. The *moderation stack* then connects these traces across policy, enforcement mechanism, interface status, user adaptation and migration, preventing one layer from standing in for moderation as a whole.

The remaining chapters extend this approach through several comparative methods. Multilingual distant reading maps moderation across adjacent conceptual fields; cross-platform speech-affordance mapping follows how an expression changes as it passes between normative environments; computational cross-media comparison traces divergence between editorial and social-media discourse; and longitudinal policy versioning and interface reconstruction make shifting thresholds visible. Chapter 5 adds *LLM perspectivism*, adapted from platform perspectivism: prompts from one normative environment are introduced into another, then compared across models, languages, risk classifiers and repeated outputs to reveal the position each system takes toward controversial speech. Word embeddings, word trees, subject–verb–object networks, alluvial diagrams and close qualitative reading support these designs rather than constituting a single method on their own.

The visuals in this thesis pursue two aims that occasionally pull in opposite directions. The first is to give justice to express the complexity and richness of platform data, particularly when it involves controversy mapping. Controversies, especially around moderation, tend to resist tidy summaries, particularly when they involve fairly unstable actors and ideas over time, oftentimes more nuanced than sociological and political categorizations propose. This is why several chapters reach for forms built to carry distributional and relational nuance, such as alluvial (or Sankey) diagrams that trace relations between multi-categorical data; beeswarm plots that can show individual posts arrayed over time rather than collapsed into averages or categories; word trees and word-embedding maps that make visible how user discussions branch through contested terms. 

The second aim is to render legible the workings of moderation systems themselves. Moderation is, by design, a process that prefers to remain unseen. The thesis treats this invisibility as something to be remapped, or “reverse-engineered”. A timeline of a platform’s evolving COVID-19 policy makes a moving target hold still long enough to be examined; a beeswarm of flags and suspensions shows when, how often, and against what a platform intervene. In some cases, reconstructions take the form of an interactive web visualization to allow a reader to traverse a full dataset for other potential conclusions outside of those made by a given a chapter. In this register, the visualization serves as partial evidence of what remains after moderation.

Finally, many of the studies in this thesis received ethical scrutiny through peer-reviewed journal assessment but did not undergo a formal internal university ethics review. When these studies were designed, the projects were understood as research on public-facing platform texts that did not collect demographic, gender, personal or other identifying data, and the methods already embedded measures intended to minimize risks to users. 4CAT, for example — a tool I use on various occasions for data collection and analysis — replaces user identifiers with hash values; when I collected data myself, I applied the same procedure, removed personal information and reported results in aggregate or as anonymized textual examples. The object of analysis was the expression and circulation of ideas and norms, quantified and qualified into topical clusters, rather than the people behind them. This explains why formal review was not sought at the time; it does not imply that public availability by itself resolves the ethical questions involved. Under current expectations for platform research, the reuse of public posts, cross-platform tracing and comparative prompting would warrant prospective institutional review, a documented assessment of contextual integrity and renewed attention to whether quoted material can be searched back to its author.

### The writing process

This is an article-based thesis. It assembles a body of empirical and conceptual work produced from roughly 2019 (before the exact start of a PhD program) to 2025. 

The intellectual trajectory tended to run from the particular to the general. It began with concrete studies on how normative or epistemic disagreements result in “platform ruptures” [@dekeulenaarAltpediasPartisanEpistemics2019]; how moderation is practiced (via deplatforming, demotion or other) and how it transforms specific platform uses (e.g., conspiracy theorization) [@dekeulenaarDeplatformingDemotionFolk2021a]; how moderation systems constitute “speech affordances” [@dekeulenaarAffordancesExtremeSpeech2023] and mold the itineraries of deplatformed content across platforms [@dekeulenaarAffordancesReplacementNarratives2023]; how policy concepts are shaped [@dekeulenaarModulatingModerationHistory2023a]; how moderation intervened in public health confusions over COVID-19 [@keulenaar6TwitterAccidental2023]; how, after a series of public crises, emerging platforms changed their underlying moderation philosophies [@dekeulenaarTwitterDemotionCommunity2025]; and how speech norms are implemented in new information management systems like LLMs [@dekeulenaarLLMsGenerationModerate2025]. [[#Chapter 6. Moderation in crisis|Chapter 6. Moderation in crisis]] is a study presented at the Association of Internet Researcher’s Conference in 2022, and [[#Chapter 10. Platform ruptures and the formation of online counter spheres|Chapter 10. Platform ruptures and the formation of online counter spheres]] a presentation given at a workshop at the University of Copenhagen in 2024. [[#Chapter 1. Censorship and moderation|Chapter 1. Censorship and moderation]] is an updated version of a presentation given to a conference on censorship in 2023, in Lisbon, which later became a book chapter [@zotero-item-46680]. 

The conceptual apparatus presented in the opening pages is, in that sense, a retrospective synthesis: it names what the case studies, taken together, turned out to be about.

### A note on bibliographic readings

Like many debates in new media studies, those on content moderation tend to have their center of gravity in the United States. Though that might be a natural effect of the overbearing presence of US scholarship in communications and media studies, the result is a tendency to universalize a particular legal and cultural settlement around free expression, free speech controversies and protections of minorities as articulated by American notions of identity and demographic history. 

Though it might be positioned in anti-hegemonic intellectual traditions, this logic tends to be hegemonic by virtue of the space it takes in scholarly debate, the power of its agenda setting, and the lack of non-English sources — conceptual or otherwise — it tends to more or less inadvertently omit. I try, where possible, to introduce international literature from Francophone, Lusophone, German, and other scholarly and legal traditions, or cases situated in Brazil and the Netherlands, to reflect histories of speech regulation bound by fairly different processes. An example is in [[#Chapter 9. Normative dislocation|Chapter 9]], which makes the point of “normative dislocation” given a specific process of re-democratization in Brazil posterior following its military regime. 

### Transition

With these orientations in place, I turn first to the conceptual question on which the rest depends. Before one can study how moderation constitutes platforms, fractures into crises, or reorganizes the geography of online speech, one needs a working account of what moderation *is*, and of how it differs from — and overlaps with — the older practices of censorship and debate management it has come to resemble. That is the task of [[#Part I. What is moderation?|Part I. What is moderation?]].
