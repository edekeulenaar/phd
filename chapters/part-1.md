# Part I. What is moderation?

Thus far in its history, moderation has often been thought of in relation to platforms. Externally, it is often described as what platforms do when they intervene in what their users say, on whose authority, within which legal frameworks, and to what extent they may be held responsible for what they leave standing [@klonickNewGovernorsPeople2017; @gorwaWhatPlatformGovernance2019b]. Internally, it is a question of institution — or, how a set of dispersed housekeeping tasks acquired a corporate nature as trust and safety departments, community guidelines, classifier thresholds, outsourced review labor and the transparency reports that account for it [@robertsScreenContentModeration2019; @gillespietarletonCustodiansInternet2018]. Between the two, moderation is most times a function they perform, well or badly, upon a stream of user-generated content. 

The difficulty with platform-based definitions is that it often means thinking about moderation in platforms’ own terms [@gillespieFactContentModeration2023]. Yet, the controversies that make moderation contentious are often greater than the actors now charged with it. One aspect of such controversies is historical. Political communities debate how language ought to serve the cause of societal and cultural reforms through neologisms intended to create or facilitate new social realities, or through the revision of terms no longer thought appropriate [@mcintoshLanguageTrumpEra2020; @greenLanguageLullabiesRussification1997]. Through language, a collective decides the terms of its relations and the terms of its dialogue; it decides, too, what may come to be said, and under what conditions a thing said may be heard as legitimate. These terms act as levers that filter, remove, marginalize, reorganize, contextualize and otherwise handle speech based on more or less agreed norms. 

Framed this way, debates over how to handle speech are not preliminaries to the question of moderation, but that question arriving under other terms. Moderation may be recognized more generally as a means to manage speech or information according to normative criteria within a longer history of normative information management techniques. There are, of course, techniques other than removal: platform policies speak of filters, contextualization labels, flagging, demotion, quarantining and rate limits. But stepping outside this terminology altogether, another technique that most readily comes to mind — now for controversial reasons — is censorship, at least in the perception of moderation as a suppressive mechanism [@rbadouardPlateformesNouveauxCenseurs]. Another is moderation as a form of debate management, which is indeed among the earliest definitions of moderators as “steering” rather than “driving” an exchange toward a fruitful end [@brochetEffectiveModerationComputer1986]. 

So what is moderation, actually? To recognize it outside platform-specific studies requires an interdisciplinary literature that situates it among other mechanisms by which speech is or has been managed. In this part, [Chapter 1. Censorship and moderation](#/chapter-1) is an attempt at exploring this literature. Setting aside the normative opposition through which moderation and censorship are usually held apart, this chapter proposes a distant reading [@morettiDistantReading2013] of a corpus of 1,960 articles, books, preprints and theses on content moderation and on neighboring speech governance practices gathered across five languages and read for four conceptual components: how each publication defines the practice (“what”), to whom it attributes it (“who”), how it justifies it (“why”), and through which techniques it is carried out (“how”).  By reading these four components across adjacent fields rather than assuming a platform definition in advance, the method answers the thesis’s first sub-question — what is moderation, and how does it differ from censorship and related forms of speech management? — while supplying the common vocabulary used in the empirical parts that follow.

From the taxonomy that results comes the claim that organizes the thesis: moderation is a speech management mechanism designed for plurivocal information environments, and it contains two core functions. The first is to *enframe* and enforce the norms of debate while intervening as little as possible. It answers the problem of how to “censor without censoring” [@jansensuecurryCensorshipKnotThat1988] through modular and dynamic techniques (labeling, obfuscation, demotion and other forms of marginalization) that reduce the reach of objectionable speech until it transgresses a threshold of tolerance and may be removed. The second is to *facilitate* socio-informational coherence: the possibility for participants in an informational environment to understand one another via contextualization, mediation, redirection, fact-checking, balancing and summarization techniques. These functions are analytically distinct but have been combined in different proportions throughout the history of online moderation [@tushnetConstantTrashCollector2019].

As platforms grew in size and needed to moderate “at scale” [@gillespieContentModerationAI2020], some of the capacities initially reserved to moderators — to prompt, contextualize, mediate, redirect and otherwise modulate what is exchanged — have been relegated to information sorting mechanisms primarily designed with simpler epistemic categories like engagement or relevance. Moderation is then left to deal primarily with adjudication, i.e., sorting and post-processing user generated content as acceptable or unacceptable (or somewhere in their borderlines). When it is reduced to this function, it risks forfeiting its legitimacy and creating an imbalance that signals a deeper loss of the consensus-building conventions through which a public arrives at the norms of its own debate.

The question applies empirically as much as conceptually. When we study moderation, what objects do we study exactly? Reading the broader literature cited in [Chapter 1](#/chapter-1), one notices that methods drawn from censorship studies and from digital methods invite us to treat moderation as something more than the drafting of platform trust and safety policies, which remain the primary object of study in the field  [@katzenbachPlatformGovernanceArchives2023]. In [Chapter 2. After deplatforming](#/chapter-2), I look instead at an ensemble of moderation mechanisms as a “moderation stack” that interconnects policy drafting to the techniques through which policy is enforced, to how users react to, evade and reinterpret enforcement, and, at the far end, migrate to alternative platforms. We speak of “traces” because this is the nature of the data we capture: moderation is visible primarily through its effects, more so than explicit function (and API-accessible data).

Interdisciplinary literatures illustrate the point well. Censorship scholars and literary editors have long sought the effects of an intervention in the displacement, correction, disappearance and other residues it leaves behind [@holquistIntroductionCorruptOriginals1994; @darntonCensorshipComparativePerspective1994; @postCensorshipSilencingPractices1998]. They, too, scraped through their own interfaces looking for traces of modifications: notes left in the margin of a censored play, redactions, censor bureau policies, bleeps and black squares. The chapter proposes to make these traces an object of study by assembling a repertoire of methods for reconstructing what we term the “scene of disappearance” (or modification) of moderated content. This first means understanding how platforms and AI companies define problematic content through their policies; retaining removable content through what we call *dynamic archiving*; capturing overt traces such as flags, labels, demotion and evidence of content unavailability; reading the wording users deploy to skirt enforcement; and following content and users as they replatform on less moderated spaces.

The method described in [Chapter 2](#/chapter-2) has since been operationalized into a research tool, [MCAT](https://amsterdam-humanities-labs.github.io/MCAT/) — the Moderation Capture and Analysis Toolkit — developed with Ivan Kisjes, Frank Anemaet and Matúš Solčány [@dekeulenaarMCATModerationCapture2024]. The idea behind the tool is to afford the modularity with which moderation techniques and data access regimes change. What can be captured on a given platform in a given year is a moving target, and an instrument for studying moderation has to be able to move with it. Given a YouTube, X, Facebook or whatever dataset obtained from most social media platforms, MCAT looks for traces of removal, flagging, demotion and other metadata found through a combination of platform APIs and web scraping. These are clandestine methods for a clandestine object of study, much like the empirical study of censorship has tended to be.
