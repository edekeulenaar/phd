# Part IV. Ruptures

The literature on alternative platforms has tended to begin with the forms of *alternativity* they sustain. Some accounts describe alt-tech as a collection of subcultures organized around the promise of uncensored expression and a shared grievance against “politically correct” moderation [@zuckermanDeplatformingOurWay2021]. Others emphasize alternative communication affordances: private or encrypted sociality, different systems of visibility and more permissive conventions for speech [@rogersDeplatformingFollowingExtreme2020]. A third body of work examines alternative infrastructures, from interfaces and open-source software to cloud hosts, domain registrars and business models [@donovanParallelPortsSociotechnical2019; @siaperaAltTechPublic2023b]. Taken together, this literature shows how alternative spaces create and circulate different speech codes, markets, technical arrangements and forms of political organization.

[Part IV. Ruptures](#/part-4) asks how such alternatives are created through the moderation crises described in [Part III. Moderation crises](#/part-3). It answers the fourth sub-question of this thesis — what do moderation crises produce when they become ruptures? — by taking counter-spheres, rather than the deleted item alone, as the unit of analysis. 

[Chapter 10. Platform ruptures and the formation of online counter spheres](#/chapter-10) offers a theoretical grounding for the two remaining chapters. It introduces the notion of *platform ruptures*, which it defines as a situation in which deep disagreements around moderation norms cause users to migrate and build alternative spaces. Since these norms are essentially contested, disagreement is of course an ordinary condition of moderation [@delaatCoercionEmpowermentModeration2012; @fraserRethinkingPublicSphere1990]. Rupture begins when this disagreement becomes consequential enough that the parties no longer share the premises through which it might be settled [@fogelinLogicDeepDisagreements1985]. At that point, the disputed content becomes a building block from which an alternative space is made, where it begins by affording what the original platform would not. From there, the content may be reorganized into a broader speech culture, moderation philosophy or epistemic project that competes with the former.

Beyond reconstructing the wider history of alt-tech spaces, [Chapter 10](#/chapter-10) also defines alt-tech as counter-spheres (from “counterpublics” [@fraserRethinkingPublicSphere1990]). These spheres are fundamentally relational: they are normative and technical environments formed around a disagreement that can no longer be contained within the sphere in which it arose. This notion also complicates any simple opposition between “mainstream” and “alternative” spaces. Counter-spheres often reproduce the technical affordances of the platforms from which they break while altering the norms that govern them. Ironically, they preserve and elaborate contested material while continuing to depend on mainstream platforms for visibility and circulation. And, as the transformation of Twitter into X demonstrates, their speech norms may eventually return to the center and become a mainstream in their own right. The chapter therefore provides the historical and conceptual frame through which Part IV approaches rupture as productive, as platform boundaries turn disputed speech into the basis for new epistemic, cultural and infrastructural formations.

The chapter is a summary of a presentation given to a symposium organized by the University of Copenhagen’s Microsociology of Online Deviance lab by Jakob Demant and Alessandro Moretti. It has not yet been published.

[Chapter 11. Epistemic ruptures in the encyclopedias of alternative facts](#/chapter-11) provides an example from the wiki space. Similar to [Chapter 7. Of migrants and strangers](#/chapter-7), this story offers a view into a moderation crisis of *epistemic nature* — that is, what happens when users fundamentally disagree about what constitutes valid knowledge based on normative grounds. It tells the story of how Wikipedia editors were removed from the platform after pushing for edits that alleged correlations between race and intelligence in an article about its historical controversies. These wikis forked a version of the article they preferred into another wiki, which continued to break from each other based on smaller epistemic disagreements. Each of these alternative wikis (or, “altpedias”) became projects born out of unresolved edit wars. What began as a contested entry thus became material for wikis of “alternative facts”, which sought to offer a platform — indeed, platformize — a body of knowledge considered repressed or misunderstood. The method is thus to reproduce Wikipedia’s entries and claims to authoritative knowledge while changing the epistemic norms by which that knowledge was judged. 

The chapter is an early article co-authored with Marc Tuters, Ivan Kisjes and Kaspar Beelen. Marc and I wrote the article together, while Ivan and I designed the method, and Kaspar contributed to the word2vec analyses of wiki text. Together, we organized several DMI data sprints on the broader topic of alternative wikis.

[Chapter 12. Deplatforming, demotion and folk theories of Big Tech persecution](#/chapter-12) follows the same relation after YouTube’s moderation of COVID-19 conspiracy narratives. Between April and October 2020, YouTube combined “hard” moderation, through video and account removal, with “soft” moderation, through the demotion of borderline content and the promotion of authoritative sources. The chapter reconstructs these interventions and examines how users interpreted them. The policies were effective in a limited but important sense: thousands of videos became unavailable, and search and recommendation systems did reduce the visibility of claims about 5G, microchips, depopulation and other COVID-19 conspiracy narratives. But it did so at the cost of reorganizing YouTube’s role within a broader market of conspiracy theories. Users removed from YouTube would congregate on BitChute to pursue the theorization of various conspiracies, while YouTube, to them, became a source of primary material for such theorizations posted by and for conspirators. Enforcement therefore reorganized the relation between platforms according to opposite but complementary normative criteria. 

The chapter is a journal article co-authored with Anthony Burton and Ivan Kisjes. Ivan designed the method, while Anthony and I designed the study and shared the writing.

Taken together, the chapters show how platforms compete and complement each other through different thresholds of tolerance for speech. What emerges is thus a market of platforms (and now AI models) that offer different speech affordances and possibilities for negotiating moderation norms. From a social and cultural perspective, this may speak to a multiplication of spheres with competing capacities for expression, which in turn produce different ideas. This is problematic from the standpoint of consensus-building on speech norms, which remain fundamental to the constitution of a public sphere. 

This is especially notable when moderation is expected to contribute to cultural change — under the premise that the disappearance of language allows the normalization of preferred alternatives. Cultural transformation depends on some degree of internalization: the possibility that publics understand, contest and eventually recognize why a form of speech has become unacceptable [@haddenWhatsNormMapping2016, p. 250; @rossiVarietiesNormativeConsensus1985, p. 334]. As a strategy for cultural change, enforcement alone thus displaces disagreement without necessarily transforming the norm from which the disagreement arose. This leaves the question that motivates [Part V. Modulation and consensus](#/part-5): if enforcement is not sustainable on its own, what other forms of moderation can and have platforms been adopting?
