# **Chapter 6** 
# Moderation in crisis: YouTube debates on the boundaries of acceptable speech 


Dictionary definitions of moderation tend to present it as a management of *excess*; an “avoidance of excess or extremes, especially in one’s behavior or political opinions” or the overall “action of making something less extreme, intense, or violent” [@oxfordenglishdictionaryModerationMeaningsEtymology2025]. Platforms have borrowed this definition to speak of moderation as something reducing excesses in speech, particularly violent collective behavior and institutional crises ranging from elections to political violence. As seen in [Chapter 1. Censorship and moderation](#/chapter-1), this ties moderation to measures that have long existed to restrain transgressive behaviors from disrupting public norms and measures of concord and cohesion. It is a mechanism of restraint to counter, marginalize, obfuscate, embargo or mediate objectionable information outside of public spheres.

At the same time, moderation as a measure is itself subject to a crisis. Since it is ultimately a normative practice, moderation systems have been criticized by myriad actors — from users [@panComparingPerceivedLegitimacy2022a] to political representatives and regulators [for example, @committeeonoversightandgovernmentreformCoverBigTech2025] — for exerting more or less arbitrary definitions of what constitutes acceptable speech. For one, different platforms, users and institutions define and locate excesses differently. EU regulation and UN conventions have framed moderation as a key guardrail against “misinformation”, “disinformation” or hate speech as the embodiment of historical excesses that, if normalized, may upend canonical post-war norms designed to maintain democratic integrity and human dignity. In an oppositely polarized view, others regard these efforts as a coordinated attempt to suppress marginalized political beliefs — whether historical or not — whose very censorship, they argue, confirms their validity. More than a crisis of platform legitimacy, this has contributed to a more fundamental crisis of consensus around what constitutes acceptable and unacceptable speech in the first place [@chongCompetingNormsFree2018b]. 

There have been a few destabilizing factors. For one, platforms have tended to give space to actors and ideas once relegated to the more or less invisible fringes of legacy media [@mungerRightWingYouTubeSupply2022]; see also [Chapter 8. Twitter as an accidental authority](#/chapter-8). Though there have been efforts to deplatform them again [@zuckermanDeplatformingOurWay2021], the sheer plurality and massive connectivity of opinions, equipped by generous affordances for expression, have tended to make this strategy technically and politically unsustainable. Part of this difficulty is that more speech affordances also mean a “democratization” of censorship, not in the sense that anyone can censor anything, but that platforms, communities and individual users have technical leverage to determine what one may or may not say in the spaces they carve online, including in smaller corners of centrally moderated platforms.

One implication is that, rather than focusing on content moderation *enforcement*, policy and media research concerned with the state or “health” [@twitterTwitterHealthMetrics2018] of online debate could first assess public consensus on speech norms, i.e., how they deliberate on the very terms in which to hold and moderate public debate. Fundamentally, speech norms — the more or less implicit norms that delimit what may or may not be sayable — are “essentially contested” [@delaatCoercionEmpowermentModeration2012]. If they are not held by authority, they must be held by consensus [@allanForbiddenWordsTaboo2006], which of course is never total nor stable but subject to “constant negotiation” in public, legal and political deliberation [@fraserRethinkingPublicSphere1990]. These deliberations end up modulating the boundaries of what is and is not sayable in public spheres, where a serious lack of consensus, in the form of deeply entrenched and polarized debates, can be taken as an indicator of a balkanization of platforms into a growing number of alt-tech and decentralized alternatives. 

To examine this issue, a great many studies in related fields have been interested in user perceptions of moderation. Some of these inquire how users experience [@myerswestCensoredSuspendedShadowbanned2018; @maHowUsersExperience2023] and interpret moderation [@maywormContentModerationFolk2024a], while others ask users about the legitimacy and fairness of moderation decisions [@panComparingPerceivedLegitimacy2022a; @maImNotSure2022; @caiContentModerationJustice2024]. Informed by these studies, I would like to delve instead into the *process of deliberation* that surrounds platform and user moderation. Tackling this question has meant a great emphasis on the conditions of online dialogue *about* and *within* moderation, be that providing context to disputed topics [@caplanContentContextModeration2018; @leeAddingMoreContext2020; @wilsonHateSpeechSocial2020]; reducing political content in recommendations [@weatherbedInstagramThreadsWill2024]; or embedding bridge-based algorithms [@dekeulenaarAlgorithmicDiplomacyIntegrating2018; @ovadyaBridgingSystemsOpen2023] in systems like Community Notes [@twitterhelpCommunityNotesTwitter2023; @xcommunitynotesDiversityPerspectives2025], which have now become the norm across platforms, including TikTok. 

Using digital methods [@rogersDigitalMethods2013], this case study offers a longitudinal and cross-platform analysis of how YouTube and its commenters have deliberated on what counts as unacceptable speech and which moderation techniques (canceling, deplatforming, shadowbanning, etc.) to apply. I use a variety of natural language processing (NLP) techniques to perform a “distant reading” [@morettiDistantReading2013] of what YouTube comments and platform policies consider to be unacceptable speech, and then close-read and contextualize instances in which significant divergence arose. I rely on an archive of 102 million YouTube comments posted between 2006 and 2022 [@jurgYouTubeRadicalization20082019]. Comments are filtered by keywords that refer to unacceptable speech (“racist”, “misogynistic”, “blasphemy”, etc.) and content moderation practices (“deplatforming”, “canceling”, “shadowbanning”, etc.). YouTube policies are collected via the Wayback Machine and Platform Governance Archive [@katzenbachPlatformGovernanceArchive2023]. Finally, I look at evidence of migration toward alternative platforms, such as Telegram, BitChute or Truth Social following those moments.

## **The moderation of crises**

One of the core functions of moderation, as discussed in [Chapter 1. Censorship and moderation](#/chapter-1), is to manage crises as a form of _excess_. There are, on the one hand, traditions of liberal political philosophy stemming as far back as Hobbes where the term “moderation” refers to a fundamental need for societies to maintain a relatively “circumscribed and peaceful sphere” [@rabinowitzHarmonyCitySoul2014, pp. 2–3] by restraining or minimizing political, religious or other “extremisms” as forms of “immoderation”. This first use of moderation is a form of restraint: one curbs “moral indignation and fervor in favor of complaisance and a willingness to get along for the sake of a shared interest in security […] and prosperity of the individual and of the nation” [@rabinowitzHarmonyCitySoul2014, p. 9]. Likewise, platform moderation is widely known to be a method of *restraint*: it demotes and, to an extent, removes what it perceives to be the worst excesses of user-generated content relative to a given norm. Here, “crises” in the form of public scandals [@hillDefinitelyNotBusiness2024] and “harmful content” [@decookSafeHarmGovernance2022b] are addressed by lowering the amplitude of expression that threatens baseline coordination.

In another sense, moderation is also synonymous with sustainable relations as a way to prevent conflict as crisis. The underlying method, here, is to facilitate concord or “social cohesion” through “agreement and shared opinion” in such a way that excesses resulting from poor social relations are unlikely to arise [@rabinowitzHarmonyCitySoul2014, pp. 217–218]. Rabinowitz draws from Montesquieu’s “spirit of pluralism” as a guarantee of moderation in “citizens themselves” via a “spirit of commerce” [@rabinowitzHarmonyCitySoul2014, p. 8; @craiutuVirtuesPoliticalModeration2001]. This implies a pluralistic environment in which moderation is practiced in the very relations among publics and the possibilities that they create for an environment “respectful of difference” [@smithSociologiesModeration2013]. Such an environment, in principle, allows for a cathartic effect that maintains concord while reserving specific spaces in society for “unleashing certain passions while redirecting and restraining others” [@rabinowitzHarmonyCitySoul2014, p. 219], moderating not only content outputs but relational dynamics over time.

Online, this speaks to early literature on content moderation as the measures necessary to maintain relative social cohesion by sustaining the conditions under which excess is less likely to arise in the first place. In the manually moderated fora that preceded generalized sorting algorithms, the moderator’s work was largely facilitative: mediating between arguments that would not otherwise meet; balancing participation so that quieter interlocutors could speak and no single perspective monopolized attention; and contextualizing exchanges by furnishing the shared references, precedents and definitions without which utterances float free of any interpretable anchor [@edwardsModeratorEmergingDemocratic2002, p. 6; @grimmelmannVirtuesModeration2015]. The aim of such interventions was less to adjudicate what could be said than to keep a plurivocal exchange intelligible and ongoing; to approximate the point at which participants might “reach understanding” by gaining access to the reasons that render their respective claims acceptable to one another [@habermasTheoryCommunicativeAction1984a, p. 230]. 

## **The crisis of moderation**

At the same time, moderation — particularly as the restraint of political or social excesses — is itself in crisis. First, there is a difference of moderation agendas, that is, disagreement over what needs moderating in the first place. The finger is often pointed at actors on the left of the political spectrum, particularly civil-society organizations pressuring platforms to sanction speech offensive to minorities [@amadieuFormesContemporainesCensure2020]. On the other side, the agenda is cast not as free expression but as a defense against “woke” overreach in its most caricatural form, or, more recently, against the incitement or glorification of violence surrounding the murder of Charlie Kirk [@u.s.supremecourtWhitneyCalifornia2741927]. The same fault line runs through users’ readings of enforcement itself: studying responses to Reddit’s quarantine policy, Shen and Rose [-@shenDiscourseOnlineContent2019a] found right-leaning users framing moderation as the suppression of political views and left-leaning users objecting that enforcement was too lax and inconsistent. These conflicting agendas rest on different historical premises about what language is of legitimate use [@mcintoshLanguageTrumpEra2020] and about what influence an expression may have on a given historical status quo, such that “excess” is refracted through incompatible normative backgrounds.

Second, there is considerable disagreement about the method of content moderation. Different estimations of how problematic an expression is lead to disagreement over how it should be punished or redressed. The deplatforming of Donald Trump is the high-profile instance, judged both proportionate, even necessary, given the violence unfolding during the Capitol Hill riots [@malkopoulouThreeModelsDemocratic2018; @hauptDemocraticSelfDefense2025], and inappropriate given his office as a sitting head of state [@douekMoreContentModeration2021]. Naturally, this has also fuelled discord over who should carry moderation decisions: Pan et al. [-@panComparingPerceivedLegitimacy2022a] find that users’ agreement with the outcome shapes perceived legitimacy more than whether a contractor, an algorithm, an expert panel or a jury made the call — that is, over who has the legitimacy to turn moderation into “levers” of public expression. For critics writing in the wake of Capitol Hill, the deeper worry is that this authority has migrated into a privatized public sphere whose criteria are opaque and unevenly applied across users, languages and jurisdictions [@philipdisalvoDeplatformingL8217attaccoCapitol2021; @mackinnonConsentNetworkedWorldwide2013], raising questions of authority, accountability and the wider locus of decision-making.

Third, and perhaps thorniest, is disagreement over how objectionable speech is itself defined. What racist, misinformative or inciting speech actually consists of is itself the subject of significant discord [@jiangTradeoffcenteredFrameworkContent2023; @oteroPlatformGovernanceSocial2024]. Legal scholarship notes that there is still no precise EU-level definition of online hate speech, leaving regulatory instruments to cover only fragments of the phenomenon [@ianroOnlineHateSpeech2023]. Where definitions are “open-textured”, as critics said of the “legal but harmful” category, the effect is to delegate to providers the decision of what harm means [@smacdonaldModeratingBorderlineContent]. This indeterminacy has direct operational consequences: automatic hate speech detection falters because annotators applying different definitions label the same content differently [@kofferDiscussingValueAutomatic]. The impact of this disagreement precedes any application of moderation, and above all the reasons given for it. Studies of contested cases find that when people simply disagree about a borderline post, a decision that satisfies one side strikes the other as illegitimate, to the point that no process, however fair, fully settles a dispute [@panComparingPerceivedLegitimacy2022a; @dliuPerceivedLegitimacyBorderline2024].

## **Essentially contested norms**

Still, disagreement over moderation is normal to the extent that speech norms are essentially contested in the sense posited by Gallie [@gallieIXEssentiallyContestedConcepts1956] and later explored by De Laat [-@delaatCoercionEmpowermentModeration2012]. Consensus over what constitutes racist, misinformative and otherwise objectionable language may enjoy more or less consensus over time [@forstToleration2017], particularly as disagreements over their definition are “not resolvable by argument of any kind” while being “sustained by perfectly respectable arguments and evidence” [@gallieIXEssentiallyContestedConcepts1956, p. 169]. This explains fairly surprising evolutions of public opinion over what is and is not acceptable: there are “endless disputes about their proper uses on the part of their users”, implicit or explicit [@gallieIXEssentiallyContestedConcepts1956, p. 169], where contestation is a feature of a concept’s application.

Speech norms are also contested in their relation to taboo. The taboo is in itself profoundly contradictory, because it signifies both “the law and its opposite” [@faugereCensureTabou2023, p. 16]; that is, it indicates the rule and that which it prohibits. For example, the expression “n-word” may indicate the rule not to say the word while, in the same breath, indicating the very thing it is trying to hide. In this sense, contestedness also appears in the very use of a speech norm. De Laat, in his study of Wikipedia editing systems, highlights the “contestedness” of moderation in the sense that it is constituted by two consistent but conflicting logics: a logic of _control_ (see [Chapter 1. Censorship and moderation](#/chapter-1)), where editors complain about moderation as a coercive and bureaucratic procedure that runs counter to Wikipedia’s egalitarian ethos of the “encyclopedia that anyone can edit”; and an _enabling_ logic, in the sense that proponents view the same rules as empowering affordances against vandalism, increasing reliability, and preserving Wikipedia. The same institutional devices thus appear both as constraint and as capacity, depending on vantage point and normative baseline.

In all cases, the contestedness is essential to use, to the extent that it is endless but not pointless. The contestedness of speech norms creates an incessant “discursive contestation” [@fraserRethinkingPublicSphere1990] that makes public spheres fundamentally “discursive spaces where society deliberates about normative standards and even develops new frameworks for expressing and evaluating social reality” [@hauserFeaturesPublicSphere1987, p. 439], keeping borders dynamic and malleable to shifting public opinion and norms rather than fixed by decree. These debates include recurring questions about what kind of language risks creating long-term violence or communal harm (e.g., hate speech); what kind of language destabilizes social or political order; ensures the dignity of individuals or groups; destabilizes a normative or historical order (e.g., Holocaust revisionism); conjures and legitimizes an unforgivable past (e.g., Nazi insignia); and, more recently, risks the deliberative manipulation of public opinion to destroy public consensus or deliberation (e.g., state-led disinformation). The breadth of these issues underscores why definitional disputes have downstream effects on enforcement, design and legitimacy.

## **Consensus features**

The tension deriving from contestedness _requires constant kinds of consensus-building_, especially in the absence of an authority [@allanForbiddenWordsTaboo2006, pp. 8–9]. Part of this is essential to the formation of the _public_ aspect of public spheres. Public spheres are constituted by “discursive features from which individuals who are personally strangers derive the code of common meanings that enable them to inhabit the same world” [@hauserFeaturesPublicSphere1987, citing Taylor 1971]. Language thus forms a “commonality” of norms and meanings that constitutes, in the terms of online territories, the “boundary” of a public sphere — the binding element of public spaces as a shared understanding — where the boundary is discursive because it is made by common meaning or, at least, by efforts to arrive at it.

It is, in this sense, a space of personal sacrifice, i.e., a “discursive realm in which individuals and groups transcend their private concerns […]” [@hauserFeaturesPublicSphere1987, p. 438]. “Public sphere” here means that, as Hauser reiterates, it becomes the space where particularities (in the form of individual, exclusive, or sector-specific interest) _must_ become universal. What ensues is the discursive work to find commonality — to “articulate the general interest of society” (Habermas 1992). Part of this exercise is to “reach understanding” [@habermasTheoryCommunicativeAction1984a, p. 230], i.e., to arrive at a universalizing function that can support coordination and mutual accountability beyond parochial frames.

On an individual or group level, actors attempt to be understood on the basis of validity claims in “communicative action”. Agents, through their utterances, make validity claims (to truth, rightness, truthfulness) on which the meaning of their utterances depends, which are then taken up by their interlocutors and form the basis of understanding and any subsequent interactions. We only understand a speech act or what someone is saying if we have access to the utterer’s reason for saying that. I.e., “We understand a speech-act when we know what makes it acceptable” [@habermasTheoryCommunicativeAction1984a], i.e., justifiable, reasonable. On a broader level, the “lifeworld” is a “background stock of cultural knowledge that is ‘always already’ familiar to agents” and thus makes mutual understanding possible, i.e., historical experiences and the meaning and context behind the normative orientation of language. This may constitute historical narratives: for example, one understands that something is antisemitic based partly on shared or told history of the Holocaust, even as normative words retain multiplicity of meaning.

Different historical periods have marked the formation of different speech norms. These include post-war events, such as denazification in Germany; social movements, such as feminist and sexual liberation movements; and nationalization or decolonization, be it external (e.g., the colonial legacy of France, England and other countries over ideas of race and identity) or internal (e.g., “derussifying” the Baltics and Eastern Europe). For example, the process of denazification in Germany, though formally delimited from 1945 to 1954, is considered an ongoing and ever-evolving process [@vincentPunirReeduquerProcess2008]. That process begins with establishing new ground rules, reforming the constitution accordingly, and establishing memorials that remind and explain why these rules were put in place. Historical pretext gives meaning to speech norms: “[…] that is, the concept implies that members of the society in question know what the norm is and agree on the strength of the obligation accompanying the rule involved” [@allanForbiddenWordsTaboo2006, p. 28]. Other times, a moral imperative is established but without full historical consensus, as in decolonization and emancipatory measures in the US.

Speech norms also have a functional impact in the form of speech affordances. One can see speech norms as forming the “pragmatic preconditions” of communication: _the rules of discourse_. These rules indicate what can and cannot be said, which in turn delimit the larger boundaries of a (linguistic) field of action, in this case a platform. Speech norms function as “rhetorical lenses that filter and alter the utterances they frame; they can accommodate some expressive modes and not others” [@fraserRethinkingPublicSphere1990]. As indicated in the previous chapter, such rules also transform into material affordances. I.e., norms around what can and cannot be said correspond to concrete affordances that may co-determine what users can and cannot do (e.g., post or repost offensive content, etc.), shaping both expression and distribution.

## **New challenges brought by platforms**

First, there is a liberalization of speech without adequate measures to mediate the norms and values different groups carry. Second, a “democratization of censorship” emerges in the sense that platforms and users are bestowed the possibility to decide what to censor in spite of platform regulations, enabled both by _personalized_ speech affordances and by the _personalization_ of speech norms (identity politics, etc.). Free speech has become a “contested norm” that no longer enjoys an elite consensus [@chongCompetingNormsFree2018b, p. 202], to the extent that it becomes an actively and reciprocally contested ground of different speech norm communities, where each community performs its own exercise of “free speech” (for example, one emphasizes limitless expressions of identity, while another emphasizes limitless expressions of ideas).

Third, we observe an acceleration of historical projects of cultural reform without adequate consensus-building, that is, without mechanisms for reaching understanding outside of authority [@habermasTheoryCommunicativeAction1984a; @allanForbiddenWordsTaboo2006]. Norms always go through different stages: emergence, cascade and internalization [@haddenWhatsNormMapping2016, p. 250]. It becomes increasingly difficult to establish a norm without internalization — the point at which there is implicit and at least “general” consensus [@rossiVarietiesNormativeConsensus1985, p. 334] — in online environments where there is no centralized point of deliberation and consensus-building. Likewise, _previous_ norms may also lose consistency, with increased debates about their historical justifications and their portability into new media contexts.

Fourth, “context collapse” impairs mutual understanding. We only understand a speech act if we have access to the utterer’s reason for saying that. I.e., “We understand a speech-act when we know what makes it acceptable” [@habermasTheoryCommunicativeAction1984a], i.e., justifiable, reasonable. With collapsed contexts, it is difficult to see the background, context and reason behind someone else’s utterance. The challenge is, thus, that interlocutors no longer share the very norms of language, which renders communication impossible, because it removes the necessary context for communication and eventual understanding — we no longer “know what makes it acceptable” normatively.

Fifth, the application of moderation decisions is partial. Studies that look into the relationship between users and platforms have focused on how users experience [@myerswestCensoredSuspendedShadowbanned2018; @maHowUsersExperience2023] and interpret moderation [@maywormContentModerationFolk2024a]. Others ask users about the legitimacy and fairness of moderation decisions [@panComparingPerceivedLegitimacy2022a], examining experiences and perspectives as misunderstandings, miscalculations, or flawed “imaginaries”. In the absence of clear (and trusted) justifications for moderation, users effectively draw “connections between related phenomena, developing non-authoritative conceptions of why and how their content was removed” [@myerswestCensoredSuspendedShadowbanned2018, p. 8]. This raises the question of whether moderation “works” if users do not consent to it in the long term, and how it transforms the content that was originally sanctioned (see e.g., [Chapter 12. Deplatforming, demotion and folk theories of Big Tech persecution](#/chapter-12)).

These studies, however, cannot highlight the overall _deliberation_ that undergirds experiences or perceptions of moderation — namely, _what_ users themselves may consider as objectionable information and how they think such information should be moderated in relation to their environment, i.e., how the platform does moderation. This is the reason why we ought to look at the _process of deliberation_ of these standards, especially when it is centralized by platform moderation. Accordingly, we follow the _flow_ of speech norms in acknowledgment of discursive spaces as “open-ended” [@hauserFeaturesPublicSphere1987, p. 439] and variable, dynamic, and in constant negotiation. This larger process of public deliberation about speech norms is of historical weight: they are, as Fraser would put it, processes that come to delineate public spheres and their “counter” parts.

# **Research questions**

How have YouTube, Twitter and Facebook publics defined acceptable speech in relation to truth and discrimination between 2006 and 2022; to what extent have these platforms’ content moderation policies (and techniques) converged with users’ definitions; what is the impact of any eventual divergence between users and platforms; and what is the role of speech norms in the formation of public spheres?

# Method

To illustrate this, I would like to see how speech norms have evolved within platform policies and their user base.

## Datasets

### YouTube comments

The YouTube dataset used for this study was collected by Dutch journalist Dimitri Tokmetzis in the fall of 2018 and later expanded in 2023. The dataset contains 600,000 videos by around 940 US and European channels of varying political orientations, in addition to 120 million video comments. We chose to examine comments because they offered the most continuous site in which users explicitly defined, disputed and evaluated speech norms; videos and transcripts were retained as contextual material but were not treated as equivalent expressions of audience deliberation.

Tokmetzis used the YouTube API v.3 to capture the YouTube channels of left- and right-wing political parties, media organizations, NGOs, and think tanks identified on Wikipedia, in academic literature, on imageboards (4chan/pol, 8chan), and reports by NGOs such as Hope not Hate and Kafka [@kafkaKafkaAntifascistResearch2021; @tokmetzisRadicaliseringOpYouTube2019]. Ranging from 2006 to late 2018, Tokmetzis’ dataset contains 1,491,099 videos, 496,858 video transcripts, and 39,755,848 comments.

It was collected in the fall of 2018 in the context of contentious debates about speech norms, including free and hate speech, or the standards by which one ought to judge something or someone as racist, misogynistic, or otherwise discriminatory [@balkinFreeSpeechTriangle2018]. 

### YouTube policies

For comparison, all of YouTube’s policies were collected using the Wayback Machine and the Platform Governance Archive (PGA for short) [@katzenbachPlatformGovernanceArchives2023]. While the latter offers complete access to policy documents, it merges them into one category, “Community Guidelines”. Thus, the Wayback Machine was used to collect each policy — from Hate Speech, to Spam or various blog posts — individually. This was done by downloading the text of all YouTube policy URLs in the PGA [@platformgovernancearchivePGAWorkbenchURL2023] using a command line. 

## Filtering process

The YouTube dataset was filtered to only contain references to debates about what constitutes acceptable language (“you can’t say *”; “* is unacceptable”, “* should be suspended OR removed OR banned OR canceled”, etc.); moderation (“suspended”, “demoted”, “shadowbanned”...); discrimination (“hate”, “harassment”, “discrimination”, “antisemitism”...); and fakeness (“fake news”, “misinformation”, “disinformation”, and so on) (see **[Table 1](#^table-1)**).

## Analysis

### YouTube policies 

The analysis consisted of two parts. The first was to look at which kinds of content YouTube policies tended to be invested in. This was done by using the relative number of edits that each policy received, in addition to which enforcement techniques were most mentioned over time (**[Figure 2a](#^figure-2a)**). Though it is not a robust indicator of what YouTube may or may not deem problematic, it does reveal a process of *problematization* of user-generated content, as seen in its identification, definition and the severity of its punishments. This process may respond to internal concerns [@yorkSiliconValuesFuture2022] or be steeped in strategic PR exercises in response to user or regulatory concerns about content moderation [@hillDefinitelyNotBusiness2024]. 

### YouTube comments 

The second was to “distant read” [@morettiDistantReading2013] the ways in which YouTube commenters discuss notions of acceptability and truth, and of how platforms should or should not moderate. This was done by using bigrams, i.e., words that are often positioned next to the term “unacceptable” in a context window of at least five other words. To make sense of results, Rieder’s Rank Flow visualization application [@bernhardriederRankFlow2019] was used to count which entities, issues and topics were most mentioned every year between 2006 and 2022 (**Figures 3 to 6**). 

It is important to highlight what is meant by “distant reading”, here. In one sense, we are not looking for explicit expressions of belief — i.e., what users *think* is or is not unacceptable — but what topics and issues arise when users discuss unacceptability in general. As the findings below show, these include crime; violent or offensive language; misinformation in the form of distortions, manipulations of context, conspiracy theories or lies; and speech moderation itself. In another sense, we are more interested in *fluctuations* in these associations than in representativeness. Comments discussing unacceptability form a small subset of the full corpus, but they illustrate considerable topical variation, particularly in relation to different events.

Next, we looked at how commenters discussed YouTube’s content moderation, or moderation in general. This implied filtering comments for sentences containing the expressions “YouTube should”, “YouTube should remove” or “YouTube should allow”, and then finding prominent themes in the ending of each sentence. We used word trees [@wattenbergWordTreeInteractive2008] for this task. The technique finds sentence completions for common prefixes and guided the close reading that follows rather than serving as a standalone result. As we found diverging ideas of what and how YouTube ought to moderate, we also examined how users conceptualized YouTube as a space, comparing sentences that mentioned “YouTube is” over time (Figures **[8](#^figure-8)** and **[9](#^figure-9)**).

# Findings

## Policies: conceptual and technical complexification

Looking at YouTube, we see that its policies, like Twitter’s (see [Part V. Modulation and consensus](#/part-5)), have evolved from broad but stable definitions of objectionable information into more detailed clauses. Elsewhere I have called the former a “constant extreme”: content that is consistently sanctioned no matter the context in which policies are enforced. This includes sex and nudity, “shocking and disgusting content”, dangerous illegal acts, pedophilia, threats, and in compliance with basic law, copyright, privacy and impersonation (see **[Figure 2a](#^figure-2a)**). 

Then, from 2014 onward, policies undergo a period of conceptual refinement, or complexification [@passmannHealthyCommunitiesToxic2023b]. Technically, the Community Guidelines page fragments into pages dedicated to each type of sanctioned object: hate speech, spam, violent or graphic content, harmful or dangerous content, and content deriving from salient events, such as the COVID-19 crisis and elections. In this context, enforcement techniques begin to move from a plethora of deletion methods to dynamic demotion. This technique is explicitly applied to what the platform calls “borderline content” [@youtubeFourRsResponsibility2021], which denotes conspiratorial, hateful or other content with varying levels of objectionability, depending on context, public or regulatory opinion.

## Comments: from unacceptable speech to unacceptable speech moderation

How do these policies compare to users’ discussions about problematic speech? In **[Figure 2b](#^figure-2b)**, we see that different issues are given slightly different levels of attention. The most discussed type of objectionable content is racism (or racists, depending on the term ending), followed by abuse / abusive, bigot / bigoted, discrimination / discriminate, sexist / sexism and offensive / offensiveness. The persistence of racism as the most mentioned term among filter keywords reveals, at least partly, its importance in wider (English-speaking) debates on speech norms. One could see it as a sort of “anchoring issue” that upholds both *debate* about speech norms and the normative imperatives that derive from it.

# Discussion

The findings return us to the distinction between a crisis that is moderated and a crisis *of* moderation. The first concerns excesses that a platform attempts to restrain. The second arises when users no longer agree on what counts as excess, who may define it, or how it should be treated. Research on moderation legitimacy has often examined whether users perceive particular decisions as fair [@panComparingPerceivedLegitimacy2022a; @maImNotSure2022]. The material here suggests that the dispute increasingly precedes a moderation decision, as YouTube commenters move from discussing unacceptable behavior, to unacceptable speech, and then to unacceptable ways of moderating speech. In this sense, what comes into crisis is the normative framework through which any decision could be accepted as legitimate.

## The rhythm of speech norms

The results can first be understood through the different *rhythms* at which speech norms change. We have seen that some prohibitions appear sedimented — some are relatively constant and others remain more susceptible to contestation. Murder, rape, terrorism, violence against civilians and child abuse, for example, recur as stable examples of unacceptability whose condemnation requires little defense. In the language of norm formation, these prohibitions approximate internalization: the point at which a norm has acquired sufficiently general consensus to operate without being justified anew in every exchange [@haddenWhatsNormMapping2016, p. 250; @rossiVarietiesNormativeConsensus1985, p. 334].

On the other hand, racism, antisemitism, sexism, discrimination, hate speech, misinformation and harmful speech move differently. Commenters dispute where these categories begin and end, what they include and whether they are applied consistently. Their persistence does not indicate that the categories are empty but that they remain essentially contested: their proper use generates “endless disputes” sustained by arguments that cannot be conclusively settled by definition alone [@gallieIXEssentiallyContestedConcepts1956, p. 169]. 

YouTube’s policy development intervenes in these different rhythms. Its early policies concentrate on relatively stable extremes; after 2014, they divide objectionability into increasingly specific categories and enforcement options. This is consistent with the conceptual complexification of platform rules described by Paßmann and colleagues [@passmannHealthyCommunitiesToxic2023b]. Yet, formal precision should not be mistaken for normative settlement: a policy can define hate speech or misinformation in greater detail while users continue to disagree over the histories, harms and examples that give those categories meaning. 

## From unacceptable speech to unacceptable ways of moderating speech 

The platform and its users do not necessarily move through these debates in synchrony. YouTube’s policies progressively formalize definitions of objectionability and distinguish among removal, restriction and demotion. Commenters, meanwhile, continue to contest categories that the platform must operationally treat as settled enough to enforce. This is not only a gap between policy and public opinion but a difference between an institution that must produce decisions and a public in which the premises of those decisions remain under negotiation. 

This difference helps explain the movement from unacceptable speech to unacceptable moderation. Early comments identify crimes, violence, terrorism, insults, racism and discrimination as the objects of concern. Later comments increasingly name censorship, deplatforming, safe spaces, political correctness, media suppression and platform intervention. Users are no longer discussing only which utterances cross a line but the very location of the line, the authority that draws it and the proportionality of the response. 

How users describe YouTube over time provides another view of the same problem. Early comments deem YouTube a “frontier”, in the sense of relatively unconsolidated and thus invisible boundaries. It is a forum, a meeting place, a source of learning and a space yet undiscovered for encountering different views. It suggests movement and an expansion of platformization and the attraction of new audiences [@kristofNatureFrontiersBoundaries1959, pp. 269–270]. In later ones, YouTube becomes a private company with rules, arbitrary interests and powers to remove or demote user-generated content. Enforced rules and techniques act as stricter delimiters that mark the platform’s extent and internal identity. They state the conditions of entry, circulation and exclusion and delineate what appear to be political contours.

The shift also qualifies procedural accounts of legitimacy. Pan and colleagues find that agreement with an outcome can matter more to perceived legitimacy than whether the decision was made by a contractor, an algorithm, an expert panel or a jury [@panComparingPerceivedLegitimacy2022a]. The findings here show why procedure alone may be insufficient: users may disagree not only with an outcome but with the category through which the case is disputed. A dispute over whether an utterance is racist, misinformative or harmful is also a dispute over evidence, context and historical meaning [@jiangTradeoffcenteredFrameworkContent2023; @oteroPlatformGovernanceSocial2024]. 

The conflict therefore becomes one of *metamoderation* as the norms and procedures through which speech norms are themselves governed. This process is captured by Fraser, who describes public spheres as sites of continuing discursive contestation in which publics do not merely apply inherited standards but develop new ways of interpreting social reality [@fraserRethinkingPublicSphere1990]. Hauser, likewise, treats their public character as dependent on the common meanings through which strangers can inhabit the same discursive world [@hauserFeaturesPublicSphere1987]. The findings place YouTube within this process, in the sense that the crisis is not that contestation exists, but that the platform must decide while offering little common space in which the terms of the decision can themselves be contested.

## The need for common spaces of speech norm deliberation

Moderation therefore cannot be reduced to enforcement. If speech norms are essentially contested, their legitimacy cannot derive from precision alone [@gallieIXEssentiallyContestedConcepts1956; @delaatCoercionEmpowermentModeration2012]. More detailed definitions and more consistent sanctions may resolve some procedural problems, but they cannot substitute for the deliberation through which a norm acquires meaning and some degree of public recognition. In the absence of an unquestioned authority, consensus remains provisional and must be renewed through continued contestation [@allanForbiddenWordsTaboo2006; @fraserRethinkingPublicSphere1990].

This is also why context matters. Work on contextual moderation has argued that a disputed utterance cannot be judged only as an isolated content object [@caplanContentContextModeration2018; @leeAddingMoreContext2020; @wilsonHateSpeechSocial2020]. The background that makes a speech act intelligible — who speaks, to whom, in what history and with what plausible consequences — is also what allows interlocutors to understand why others regard it as acceptable or unacceptable [@habermasTheoryCommunicativeAction1984a, p. 230]. When that background collapses, the platform can still decide whether to remove, label or demote the content, but the decision cannot by itself explain or reconcile the different norms through which users interpret it.

Part of what is missing from platform governance, then, is an intermediary space between public disagreement and an enforcement decision. The findings show that users already debate what counts as hate speech, censorship, misinformation, discrimination and legitimate intervention. Yet these discussions have no stable relation to the policy systems that decide such questions: they occur beneath videos, across channels and after sanctions, while policy revision and enforcement remain elsewhere. Moderation can observe these debates as signals of controversy, but it does not ordinarily organize them as inputs into the formation or revision of speech norms.

This is the task of metamoderation. It does not mean that every decision must be put to a vote, or that platforms should wait for unanimity before acting, but that procedures must be created in which definitions, reasons, precedents and enforcement methods can themselves be examined. More recent proposals for bridging systems similarly ask how technical arrangements might surface agreement across otherwise opposed groups rather than simply reward the largest or most engaged one [@dekeulenaarAlgorithmicDiplomacyIntegrating2018; @ovadyaBridgingSystemsOpen2023]. If moderation is a means of maintaining the conditions of public communication, its long-term legitimacy depends on whether those governed by speech norms can understand, question and participate in the processes through which those norms are made.
