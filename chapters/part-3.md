# **Part III. Moderation crises**

[Part II. Speech affordances](#/part-2) examined how speech norms take form in community guidelines, moderation techniques, interface functions and user cultures which signal what may be said, by whom and under what conditions. I called these arrangements *speech affordances*, i.e., the material possibilities for expression afforded by platform speech cultures, policies and material constraints. While these affordances may appear fairly consolidated (and consolidating), we see how, in [Part III. Moderation crises](#/part-3), they hold considerable tensions in place. We have seen how enforcement practices tend to displace disputes without always resolving the disagreements that produce them ([Chapter 3](#/chapter-3); [Chapter 4](#/chapter-4)). We have also seen how the expression of more or less objectionable information — as illustrated by Reddit “controversies” ([Chapter 5](#/chapter-5)) — contain disputed, if ever “contested” [@gallieIXEssentiallyContestedConcepts1956] definitions of *what amounts as objectionable* in the first place. Such tensions are especially prescient when, in the highly dynamic environments that are social media, social norms and terminologies are susceptible to rapid change and no robust consensus-building processes. 

What happens when moderation becomes the subject of such contestations? Most literature has tended to approach moderation-related conflicts from different angles. One account treats online conflict as the result of *insufficient* moderation: the idea that the relaxation of editorial gatekeeping exposes publics to hostile actors, language and forms of informational manipulation that make communicative spaces uninhabitable. A related perspective describes a wider field of “information disorders” in which hate speech, misinformation and coordinated influence overwhelm the capacity of platforms and publics to maintain informational integrity [@socialscienceresearchcouncilHateSpeechInformation2020]. Another places responsibility on companies that respond inadequately to their societal impact. Moderation systems are read through negligence: firms have the technical capacity to intervene but fail to do so because trust and safety remains subordinate to growth, engagement, compliance or brand protection [@hillDefinitelyNotBusiness2024]. 

Here, I propose to look at moderation as the very subject of public disputes, particularly between platforms, users and institutional actors. I call these disputes “crises”, or instances in which the purpose and legitimacy of moderation is called into question by disagreements about its normative premises. These disagreements can form around three contested questions: which threshold of tolerance should govern a debate; what counts as acceptable speech, and for which historical or normative reasons; and which actors, institutions and contexts are entitled to decide. A crisis tends to begin when the actors involved no longer share the premises through which the disagreement might be worked through, which Fogelin calls a *deep disagreement* [@fogelinLogicDeepDisagreements1985]. This absence of *metamoderation* processes — appeals, consultations and collective procedures through which the terms of moderation can themselves be discussed and agreed upon — makes such a crisis more likely to harden into ruptures [@geigerBotbasedCollectiveBlocklists2016b, p. 20; @seeringModeratorEngagementCommunity2019], which occurs when actors begin to leave, exclude one another or build elsewhere. The disputed content then becomes part of the normative and technical basis of an alternative space like alt-tech spaces, which I explore in [Part IV. Ruptures](#/part-4).

The four chapters in this part examine moderation crises across different relations: between users and a platform in [Chapter 6](#/chapter-6); between social media audiences and editorial media in [Chapter 7](#/chapter-7); between a platform and the public authorities it is expected to follow in [Chapter 8](#/chapter-8); and between global platform policies and a local process of historical accountability in [Chapter 9](#/chapter-9). [Chapter 6. Moderation in crisis](#/chapter-6) examines how YouTube moderation policies (and practices) diverged from users’ conceptions of objectionable language between 2006 and 2022, via the comment section. The comparison finds that both YouTube and user conceptions of objectionable information are subject to frequent (and frequently contested) changes, while some notions remain — as highlighted later in [Chapter 13. Modulating moderation](#/chapter-13) — consistently unaccepted (e.g., murder and pedophilia). Those subject to change include racism, antisemitism, discrimination, hate speech and misinformation, under competing definitions (for example, racism as the fact of qualifying skin colors versus that of a structural inequality between socially-defined identities; or, as of late, antisemitism as opposition to Zionist philosophy versus the hatred of Jewish people). 

Each of these definitions encapsulates different historical premises, which in themselves constitute a historical consensus. A divergence of definitions is a divergence over the very premises of what constitutes objectionable language and why. When it occurs, commenters move from discussing unacceptable speech to unacceptable *ways of moderating speech*. Censorship, deplatforming, shadowbanning and platform authority become objects of controversy in their own right.

[Chapter 7. Of migrants and strangers](#/chapter-7) focuses on the material impacts of moderation crises. It asks what happens when journalistic editorial norms — which constitute speech norms in their own right — diverge with those of their social media audiences, particularly how this impacts users’ expectations of what can and cannot be reported as a journalistic fact.  

As a case study, it compares Dutch television coverage of the European refugee crisis with the responses of its Facebook and Twitter audiences between 2013 and 2018. It finds that, while television coverage remained descriptive, attending to institutions, routes, countries and political responses — sections of its social media audience increasingly spoke of migration through the language of invasion, cultural threat and criminality. This created a tension over speech norms, where the editorial norms of broadcasters were called into question by users contesting the journalistic definitions of anti-xenophobic language. This became especially prevalent after the sexual assault of New Years 2015, in Cologne, where local German coverage initially did not report the ethnic origins of perpetrators because of editorial norms against discrimination. 

A clash over speech norms had direct effects in what media users chose to consume. As users found the boundaries of broadcasting norms too limiting to report what they perceived to be valid facts, they then compared or complemented mainstream coverage with that of Breitbart and GeenStijl, both alternative outlets. These, in turn, offered users a form of “transgressive” reporting specifically focused on factualities that mainstream editorial norms would not consider reportable. This crisis was thus *both* normative and epistemic, formed around how competing norms define what is reportable in the first place.

[Chapter 8. Twitter as an accidental authority](#/chapter-8) examines a crisis of epistemic authority during the first year of the COVID-19 pandemic. Platforms attempted to contain misinformation by directing users toward “authoritative sources” on transmission, treatment and prevention. Twitter, in particular, centralized access to public-health guidance, raised authoritative information and labeled claims that tended to contradict it. This strategy depended on those authorities providing a sufficiently coherent account of what was known. The WHO, CDC, NIH, PAHO and the White House, however, changed their guidance over time and contradicted one another on masks, hydroxychloroquine and modes of transmission, leaving their audiences divided around those contradictions. 

Twitter’s policy nevertheless required a boundary between trustworthy and misleading information, thus creating a paradox: it had organized moderation around deference to authority, but could no longer defer when authorities contradicted one another. In this situation, it responded by acting upon the degree of consensus among them, flagging or sanctioning outliers rather than establishing the objective truth of each claim (Trump being the case in point). This decision-making placed Twitter in a situation once described by Carl Schmitt in *The Concept of the Political* [@schmittConceptPolitical1996]: that the sovereign is that who takes action in a state of exception — here, where public (health) authorities lack consensus on their governance guidelines. As a result, Twitter steps in as an authority in its own right, even if just an accidental one.

[Chapter 9. Normative dislocation](#/chapter-9) is the culminating case of a crisis between Brazilian institutional actors and the moderation systems of US-based platforms around the 2022 presidential election and the attacks on the Three Powers Plaza on 8 January 2023. At the time, Brazil was undergoing a string of institutional crises when then-President Bolsonaro aligned heavily on the idea of a military intervention in case of supposed electoral fraud. Such propositions were accompanied by a surge and relative normalization of militaristic content online, which consisted in large part of advancing revisionist narratives about the role and actions of the Brazilian army during the military dictatorship of 1964-1980. This form of content — which in the US and Europe would equate in kind to WWII revisionist accounts — was treated by platforms as “electoral misinformation” developed in the context of the 2020 US election. 

This mismatch interrupted a longer historical process of accountability. Since the end of military rule, amnesty, reparation, truth commissions, constitutional law and public memory have constituted an attempt to turn contested experiences of the military regime into a national experience of transitional justice and reconciliation. In their own way, these efforts consisted of moderation mechanisms as the management and facilitation of public historical memory, particularly by establishing a common vocabulary of human rights, the documentation of political violence and renegotiating the place of the armed forces in public life. Such processes were never complete, but they supplied a historical basis from which calls for a new military intervention could be understood as infringements against a collective memory.

Marcelo Alves dos Santos Junior and I call the platform mismatch a *normative dislocation*: a situation whereby moderation system based upon foreign norms ceases to participate in a local process of norm formation. By treating militaristic speech primarily as electoral misinformation, or by failing to recognize it as an object of moderation, platforms contradicted and partially derouted the Brazilian process of accountability.

The cases described by each of these chapters do not necessarily insist that every moderation crisis should be resolved through consensus, but why the absence of metamoderation matters as a space to clarify definitions, the historical premises, the procedures and rules that constitute moderation. Where no such space is viable, the affordances of one medium may become intolerable to some of those who inhabit it. [Part IV. Ruptures](#/part-4) follows this process.
